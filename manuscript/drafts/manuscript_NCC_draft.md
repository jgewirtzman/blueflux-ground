## Abstract (~160 words)

Mangroves are among the most carbon-dense ecosystems on Earth, but they also emit methane. Because high salinity is thought to suppress methanogenesis, saline-mangrove methane is treated as negligible in IPCC Tier-1 emission factors and is largely absent from greenhouse-gas budgets. Whether this assumption holds across all emitting pathways, and how methane responds as tropical cyclones kill mangroves, has not been established. Here we show, by combining component-resolved chamber fluxes across six ecosystem pathways, scaled to stand level with terrestrial laser scanning and evaluated against airborne eddy covariance along a hurricane-disturbance gradient in the Florida Everglades, that intact saline mangrove emits more methane than emission factors imply, distributed across soils, tidal water and stems. Hurricane-driven mortality amplifies methane emission and reverses the porewater salinity-methane relationship, from negative to positive. Expressed as radiative forcing, the ecosystem changes from a net sink to a net source, and methane rises from ~2% to ~36% of the combined forcing on a 20-year horizon. These results indicate that methane emission factors and blue-carbon accounting should incorporate baseline saline-mangrove emission and disturbance state.

## Introduction (~550 words)

Mangrove forests store 2-10 Pg of carbon and accumulate 18-23 Tg C yr-1 across ~147,000 km2 of tropical and subtropical coastline. This capacity has made them central to blue-carbon policy, carbon crediting and nature-based climate solutions. But like all wetlands they produce methane, whose global warming potential is 28-80 times that of CO2. Whether methane offsets mangrove carbon uptake, and under what conditions, remains a key uncertainty for climate accounting.

The prevailing view is that saline mangroves emit little methane. On the production side, tidal replenishment of sulfate and oxygen sustains a thermodynamic hierarchy in which more favourable electron acceptors are consumed before methanogenesis proceeds; on the consumption side, aerobic and sulfate-coupled anaerobic oxidation remove methane near the sediment surface. Methane offsets to carbon burial are therefore held to be far larger in freshwater wetlands (~27%) than in saline soils (~7%), and the IPCC 2013 Wetlands Supplement treats annual mangrove methane as negligible above 18 PSU. Yet this framework rests on soil measurements and fixed, biome-level factors that ignore ecosystem state. Methane escapes not only from soils but from tidal water, tree stems, roots and coarse woody debris; globally, methane may offset 17-23% of mangrove soil carbon burial, with tree stems alone contributing a further ~17%. Tree stems in particular have recently emerged as an overlooked emission pathway across wetland and upland forests, yet these pathways have rarely been measured together, and component-resolved methane budgets for intact mangroves are almost absent, particularly in the Western Hemisphere. Emerging evidence further undermines the suppression paradigm: methanogenesis occurs under sulfate-rich and even hypersaline conditions once considered prohibitive, sustained by methylotrophic methanogens that use noncompetitive substrates such as methylamines and methanol to bypass sulfate reducers, and even in oxygenated soils — indicating microbial pathways the classical redox ladder does not predict.

Mangroves are also increasingly disturbed. Tropical-cyclone intensity and the proportion of major hurricanes are rising, and storms defoliate canopy, kill roots, pond water and leave standing dead wood. Loss of live-root oxygen transport shifts sediments toward reducing conditions, decomposition of killed biomass supplies labile carbon to methanogens, and persistent flooding creates new emitting surfaces — all of which should favour methane and could break the salinity-suppression relationship. Limited observations are consistent with this: creek methane rose 45% after a Gulf of Mexico dieback, and dead stems emitted eightfold more methane than living ones in Australia. But no study has resolved how disturbance alters component-specific pathways, validated those changes against an ecosystem-scale constraint, or tested whether the salinity-methanogenesis relationship persists once a forest is killed.

Resolving this requires measurement at multiple scales: chambers to isolate pathways, structural scaling to convert flux densities to stand budgets, and an independent top-down constraint to validate them. Here, as part of the NASA BlueFlux campaign, we quantify component-resolved methane across a hurricane-disturbance gradient in the Florida coastal Everglades, where Hurricane Irma (2017) caused extensive mortality. We combine static chambers on six ecosystem components, terrestrial laser scanning for surface-area quantification, eddy-covariance towers and the CARAFE airborne eddy-covariance instrument, across intact, regenerating and ghost forest. We construct the first component-resolved, closure-validated methane budgets for hurricane-disturbed mangroves and test whether mortality switches these systems from radiative cooling to warming.

## Results

### Component-resolved methane, even in intact saline mangrove (Fig. 2)

We analysed 752 methane and CO2 fluxes from six ecosystem components across eight sites and three seasonal campaigns (dry March 2022, wet October 2022, dry March 2023; Fig. 2a,b). Intact saline mangrove was not a negligible methane source: soils, tidal water and stems all emitted, and summed across pathways their emission substantially exceeds the near-zero flux that salinity-based emission factors assign above 18 PSU. Across all sites, water surfaces and soils had the highest mean CH4 flux rates (water 17.6 nmol m-2 s-1, 95% CI 10.3-26.5, n = 47 floating-chamber placements; soils 17.1, 10.2-25.5, n = 118), followed by stems (8.0, 4.8-12.1, n = 476) and roots (3.5, 1.2-7.8, n = 65); coarse woody debris was lower (1.0, 0.0-2.6, n = 27) and leaf fluxes near zero (0.02, 0.01-0.04, n = 19). Emission was near-universal: 93% of stem and 91% of root fluxes, and 100% of water-surface fluxes, were positive (net emission to the atmosphere).

Disturbance elevated stem, soil and water-surface emission (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Table 2). Stem CH4 in ghost (11.8 nmol m-2 s-1, 7.5-17.4) and regenerating (14.3, 3.3-33.9) forest exceeded intact mangrove (0.29, 0.19-0.41) by 41- and 50-fold; soil CH4 rose from 5.9 (intact) to 11.7 (ghost) and 55.6 (regenerating); and water-surface CH4 from 0.9 (intact) to 16.2 (ghost) and 56.9 (regenerating). Emission was strongly seasonal: wet-season stem CH4 (14.7, 7.1-25.5) was 4.2-fold the dry-season value (3.5, 2.2-5.0), and wet-season water-surface CH4 (44.8, 28.9-64.7) 9.3-fold (4.8, 3.7-6.0), reflecting inundation — ghost sites had standing water in both campaigns (dry-season levels lower but not drained), emitting through the flooded water surface; the ghost budget is insensitive to whether the dry-season surface is treated as water or exposed soil (Supplementary Text S.T2). Ebullition occurred mainly on wet-season water surfaces at disturbed sites (bubbles in 6 of 15 wet-season and 5 of 32 dry-season placements), contributing up to 34% of water-surface methane (34.0% at CP40, 3.9% at BL60; 14% overall; not separable at FLM30, where the Picarro was used; Supplementary Fig. 1). Stem CO2 was a net source across all classes (2.6 umol m-2 s-1, 2.3-2.9, n = 476), and leaves were the only component with net CO2 uptake (-1.5, -2.3 to -0.8, n = 19).

### The role of trees (Fig. 3)

Tree stems were a spatially structured methane pathway. Per-area flux declined steeply with height on the stem (p < 0.001; Fig. 3a,b): modelled Rhizophora mangle wet-season flux fell from 1.10 nmol m-2 s-1 at the sediment surface to 0.30 at 100 cm — a transport signature of soil-derived gas venting through the lower bole rather than in-situ production — and this decline was steeper and began from a higher base in disturbed forest; stem CO2 showed no height dependence (p = 0.93; Fig. 3d,e). Species identity affected flux (p = 0.02), with a significant species x height interaction (p < 0.001; Fig. 3c): at 0-50 cm Laguncularia racemosa emitted most (4.04 nmol m-2 s-1, 1.61-9.60), and across all heights it exceeded R. mangle (1.05 vs 0.44, p = 0.02) and Conocarpus erectus (0.29, p = 0.05). Standing dead stems continued to emit: the dead-Avicennia germinans marginal mean (1.60) was higher than living (0.70), though not significantly (p = 0.06), and dead R. mangle did not differ significantly from living (0.59 vs 0.89; p = 0.92), indicating that transport and emission persist after tree death. These high per-area rates do not scale to the stand: because woody surface area per unit ground is small and flux concentrates near the base, stems contribute ~1% of the whole-ecosystem methane budget (0.6% ghost, 1.5% intact; Fig. 4), whereas the extensive living prop-root surfaces of intact Rhizophora forest make roots ~44% of the intact-forest budget. Per-area flux rate and stand-level contribution are therefore decoupled: the stem pathway is minor at the stand scale, whereas roots are a substantial pathway in intact forest.

### Structure-explicit bottom-up budgets (Fig. 4)

Converting flux densities to stand-level budgets requires component surface area, which we resolved with terrestrial laser scanning (Fig. 4a). Woody and aerial-root surface area collapsed with disturbance: intact plots supported large living Rhizophora prop-root systems (413-419 m2 per ~1,800-2,200 m2 plot, 0.19-0.24 m2 root per m2 ground) and abundant woody surface (0.86-0.98 m2 m-2), whereas ghost plots retained little live structure (49-58 m2 root; 0.02-0.03 m2 m-2). Soil and water areas were set by inundation.

Scaling component fluxes by these areas yields the first component-resolved methane budgets for these forests (Fig. 4b,c). Plot-scale emission was several-fold higher in ghost than intact forest (ghost mean ~29, range 7.5-70.7 mg CH4 m-2 d-1; intact mean ~2.4, range 0.9-4.7); individual ghost plots reached 70.7 (95% CI 29.8-111.5) and 28.9 (15.5-42.5) mg CH4 m-2 d-1 in the wet season. Even the intact-forest budget — ~0.9 g CH4 m-2 yr-1 class-mean, and ~1.4 at the more productive Shark River site — is far above the near-zero emission that salinity-based Tier-1 factors assign to mangroves above 18 PSU, implying that regional and global wetland methane budgets systematically omit an intact saline-mangrove source. The two states emitted through different pathways: ghost-forest emission was ~99% flooded water surface, whereas intact-forest emission was dominated by aerial roots (44%) and tidal water (35%), with soils 20%, stems 1.5% and coarse woody debris <1%. Despite their elevated per-area rates and strong height and species dependence, stems were a negligible term in both states, because woody surface area per unit ground is small and stem flux falls with height. The leading pathway is set by hydrology — standing water over dead, flooded ghost soils versus prop roots and tidal water over a mostly flooded forest floor in intact forest.

The budgets are robust to structural assumptions. Class-mean totals changed by ~4% or less between stem height-extrapolation scenarios (exponential-decay vs zero-above-maximum: ghost 29.3 vs 30.5; intact 2.43 vs 2.43 mg CH4 m-2 d-1), a consequence of the small stem term; and because ghost emission is overwhelmingly a directly measured water-surface flux scaled by inundated area, structural uncertainty is confined to the intact class, where it depends on the exposed-soil fraction and the TLS root surface area (Supplementary Figs. 5-7).

The regenerating class, though lacking TLS structure and top-down closure, had the highest component areal rates of any class (soil 56 and water 57 nmol m-2 s-1; Fig. 2a). A first-order upscaling that assigns it woody and root surface area intermediate between intact and ghost forest, at its measured inundation, yields ~33 g CH4 m-2 yr-1 — exceeding the ghost class — suggesting that early regeneration may be the peak of the disturbance methane response rather than a return toward the intact baseline (Supplementary Text S.T6). Single-visit context sites further show that these rates are setting-dependent: a Ten Thousand Islands ghost site (Marco Island) emitted an order of magnitude less than the core Everglades ghost sites at the same season (soil 2.6 vs up to 31 nmol m-2 s-1; stem 0.5 vs 11-14), whereas an additional intact site (Rookery Bay) matched the core intact class, and a scrub-mangrove ecotone showed intermediate root and low stem emission (Supplementary Text S.T8).

### A biogeochemical regime shift (Fig. 5)

Porewater chemistry differed systematically across the disturbance gradient (Fig. 5). A principal-components analysis of 11 porewater variables explained 83.5% of variance in three components; the first (40.4%) was loaded by salinity (+0.43), dissolved CH4 (+0.40), sulfate (+0.37) and d13C-CH4 (+0.36), opposed by ORP (-0.38) and dissolved oxygen (-0.36), and separated ghost and regenerating sites (high PC1) from intact sites (low PC1; Fig. 5a). Dissolved porewater methane was an order of magnitude higher at disturbed sites — highest at FLM30 (73 uM) and CP40 (36 uM) versus SRS6 (5 uM) and SRS5 (4 uM) — despite porewater salinities of 50-64 PSU at ghost sites versus 20-30 PSU at intact sites.

The salinity-methane relationship reversed with disturbance (Fig. 5b). Within intact and regenerating forest, porewater salinity and dissolved methane were negatively correlated (r = -0.48, p = 0.16) — the classic signature of sulfate suppression — whereas within ghost forest the relationship inverted to a significant positive correlation (r = 0.60, p = 0.03): methane rose with salinity. Individual ghost sites showed large seasonal swings in dissolved methane at nearly constant salinity (e.g., CP40 ranged 4-62 uM across seasons at 50-63 PSU), inconsistent with salinity control. Hurricane mortality thus overrides the redox-ladder expectation that governs methane in undisturbed saline mangroves.

### Independent closure and net radiative forcing (Fig. 6)

We next evaluated the bottom-up budgets against an independent constraint and then expressed them as radiative forcing. Airborne eddy covariance disaggregated by vegetation class (two end-members, intact and ghost; Delaria et al. 2024) provides an independent landscape flux, and it agrees with the bottom-up budgets within uncertainty — both rank ghost above intact in the wet season (top-down ghost methane 51 +/- 27 vs intact 29 +/- 16 nmol m-2 s-1; bottom-up 35.9 vs 2.0; Fig. 6a). Dry-season end-members also overlapped within one standard error (bottom-up ghost 6.4 vs top-down 4.5 +/- 10.4; intact 1.5 vs -0.2 +/- 8.6 nmol m-2 s-1), as did net CO2 exchange (intact a net daily sink, bottom-up -3.1 to -3.3 vs top-down -2.2 +/- 2 umol m-2 s-1; ghost near zero to a slight source in both; Fig. 6b; Supplementary Table 4). The one discrepancy — wet-season intact methane, where bottom-up (2.0) falls below the large-uncertainty airborne value (29 +/- 16) — remains within uncertainty. Because the methods share no measurements or parameters — chambers scaled by laser-scanned surface area versus footprint-disaggregated airborne flux — their agreement is independent of any single method's assumptions.

Expressing the validated methane budget as CO2 equivalents and combining it with net ecosystem CO2 exchange closes the radiative balance (Fig. 6c,d). Intact mangrove was a strong net sink: bottom-up net CO2 uptake (-4,427 g CO2 m-2 yr-1, equivalent to -1,208 g C m-2 yr-1) closely matched the independent tower-based net ecosystem productivity for this forest (1,170 +/- 127 g C m-2 yr-1), and overwhelmed methane emission (0.9 g CH4 m-2 yr-1; +25 g CO2-eq m-2 yr-1 at GWP100, +72 at GWP20), which offset only 0.6% (GWP100) or 1.6% (GWP20) of the CO2 sink. Ghost forest was a net source (+1,822 g CO2-eq m-2 yr-1, GWP100; +2,393 at GWP20): loss of the photosynthetic canopy turned the CO2 balance positive (+1,523 g CO2 m-2 yr-1) and elevated methane (10.7 g CH4 m-2 yr-1; +299 g CO2-eq at GWP100, +870 at GWP20) became a first-order warming agent, rising to 16.4% (GWP100) or 36.3% (GWP20) of the combined forcing (Fig. 6d).

Hurricane conversion therefore switched the ecosystem from a net radiative sink to a source — a change of ~6,200 (GWP100) to ~6,700 (GWP20) g CO2-eq m-2 yr-1. A Monte Carlo propagation of chamber, leaf-respiration and GPP uncertainty gives non-overlapping forcing intervals for the two states (intact -5,889 to -3,319; ghost +1,403 to +2,689 g CO2-eq m-2 yr-1, GWP100), so the switch is robust to these uncertainties. Applied to the area of Caribbean mangrove converted to ghost forest by recent hurricanes, this per-area switch scales to a material regional addition to greenhouse-gas forcing (Fig. 6e,f).

## Discussion (~750 words)

### Emissions from saline coastal wetlands

Measuring across all emitting pathways shows that intact, saline mangrove is a meaningful methane source — ~0.9 g CH4 m-2 yr-1 at the stand scale (~1.4 at our most productive site), from roots, tidal water, soils and stems — well above the near-zero flux that salinity-based emission factors assume at high salinity. The paradigm that governs mangrove methane derives largely from soil measurements and omits the surfaces where much of the flux actually occurs. A direct consequence is that current blue-carbon and wetland methane budgets under-count a real term even before any disturbance, biasing net climate benefits high.

Trees are one such surface. Mangrove stems were spatially and biologically structured conduits: emission declined with height, varied among species, and persisted in standing dead stems. The height decline is consistent with internal gas-phase transport of soil-derived methane through aerenchyma and lenticels; species differences are consistent with variation in wood density and vessel structure; and the persistence of emission from standing dead stems suggests that as trees lose active, root-mediated aerenchymatous transport they retain a passive conduit and a decomposition-fed source. As component fluxes are incorporated into greenhouse-gas budgets, however, per-area rates should not be equated with stand-level contributions: stems were less than 1% of the ecosystem methane budget, whereas roots — through the living prop-root surfaces of intact Rhizophora forest — were a substantial term. Component flux rates and stand-level contributions must be distinguished.

### Disturbance re-wires methane and inverts the redox ladder

Hurricane die-off amplified methane 41- to 50-fold at the stem, 2- to 9-fold in soils and 19- to 66-fold at water surfaces, and inverted the porewater salinity-methane relationship from suppression (r = -0.48) to promotion (r = 0.60, p = 0.03). The mechanism is coherent: persistent ponding, a pulse of labile carbon from killed biomass, and loss of live-root oxygen transport push flooded sediments toward methanogenesis even as salinity stays high — plausibly by favouring methanogens that exploit noncompetitive substrates and so circumvent sulfate reducers — while the flooded surface becomes the dominant conduit to the atmosphere. In effect, disturbance inverts the redox ladder: the salinity relationship that suppresses methane in intact saline mangrove becomes one that promotes it. This decoupling of methane from salinity is likely not unique to hurricanes — it should apply wherever mangroves are drowned, ponded or killed by saltwater intrusion and sea-level rise, and to ghost forests worldwide. The magnitude of the response is nonetheless setting-dependent: a ghost site in the Ten Thousand Islands emitted an order of magnitude less than the core Everglades ghost sites (Supplementary Text S.T8), so hurricane-killed mangrove spans a wide emission range rather than a single high value. The trajectory may also be non-monotonic — a first-order upscaling places the early-regenerating stand above even the ghost class (~33 g CH4 m-2 yr-1; Supplementary Text S.T6), suggesting the methane response can peak during regeneration before relaxing, though this rests on assumed structure and awaits structural and top-down constraint.

### From radiative sink to source

Loss of canopy CO2 uptake after mortality is expected; the additional result here is that methane converts that loss into a net positive forcing. Methane's share of combined forcing rises from ~0.6% (GWP100) in intact forest to 16.4% (GWP100) and 36.3% (GWP20) in ghost forest — more than a third of the forcing on the shorter horizons relevant to near-term climate targets. The full switch, ~6,200 (GWP100) to ~6,700 (GWP20) g CO2-eq m-2 yr-1, is robust in Monte Carlo (non-overlapping intervals). A degraded mangrove is therefore a methane-amplified source rather than a diminished sink.

These results argue that methane emission factors for mangroves need two revisions: a non-zero baseline for intact saline forest, and an explicit dependence on disturbance state. As intact factors are corrected upward and disturbed area grows under intensifying cyclones and rising seas, the methane term in coastal-wetland accounting becomes materially larger — and the choice of time horizon matters, since methane's share more than doubles from GWP100 to GWP20. IPCC Tier-1 factors, blue-carbon crediting and nature-based-solution frameworks, which value standing mangrove for carbon, should therefore carry both an accurate methane baseline and a permanence-risk term for disturbance. Applied across the area of Caribbean mangrove converted to ghost forest by recent hurricanes, the per-area sink-to-source switch scales to a material regional addition to greenhouse-gas forcing (Fig. 6e,f), and the mangrove area at risk under intensifying cyclones and sea-level rise makes this a growing term in regional and global wetland methane budgets.

### Conclusions

This study provides, to our knowledge, the first top-down-validated methane budget for any mangrove forest, the first component-resolved mangrove methane budget in the Western Hemisphere, and the first landscape-scale quantification of how disturbance reorganizes mangrove methane — resolving the contested role of tree stems within that budget. The agreement between chamber-plus-laser-scanning and airborne eddy covariance, across pathways, structure and the landscape, is stronger evidence than either method alone, and offers a template for other blue-carbon and disturbed ecosystems where partitioning and closure are both needed.

Several limits bound these conclusions: our forcing terms are net ecosystem exchange, not long-term carbon burial — the quantities the atmosphere experiences rather than what is permanently sequestered; the disturbance trajectory is inferred from a space-for-time gradient rather than tracked through time; two campaigns do not resolve diurnal, tidal or full seasonal cycles; the regenerating class was measured at the component scale and only approximately upscaled (Supplementary Text S.T6), not closed against a top-down constraint; ghost-forest gross primary production was treated as negligible, making the ghost source a conservative bound; and tidal export of dissolved inorganic carbon means bottom-up respiration may legitimately exceed tower estimates. None alters the direction or robustness of the sink-to-source switch, but they identify where budgets remain incomplete — ebullition, lateral tidal transport of methane and carbon, canopy-scale fluxes, and temporal aliasing from episodic sampling — as the terms most in need of constraint.

Saline mangroves are therefore a larger and more disturbance-sensitive methane source than current accounting assumes, and hurricane mortality can flip a mangrove from a net radiative sink to a methane-amplified source. Closing this gap will require baseline and disturbance-dependent emission factors across salinity and geomorphic settings, a full carbon budget that includes soil burial and lateral tidal fluxes, and the microbial metagenomes that link the geochemical regime shift to its drivers — priorities made more urgent as tropical cyclones intensify and seas rise across the world's mangrove coastlines.

## Methods

### Study system and disturbance gradient

We worked in mangrove forests of the southwest Florida coast within and adjacent to Everglades National Park, across three condition classes along a hurricane-disturbance gradient: intact, early-regenerating and ghost forest. The two ghost-forest sites (FLM30, CP40) experienced extensive tree mortality when Hurricane Irma (September 2017) drove storm surge and drowning across the region; five years later they retained standing dead trunks and root structures with no canopy recovery. The regenerating site (BL60) was also damaged by Irma but supported active seedling and sapling recruitment beneath a low, discontinuous canopy. The two intact sites (SRS5, SRS6) are tall riverine forests along the Shark River Slough that sustained comparatively less structural damage. The dominant species are *Rhizophora mangle*, *Avicennia germinans*, *Laguncularia racemosa* and *Conocarpus erectus*, whose composition varies with geomorphic position, tidal connectivity and disturbance history. The climate is subtropical with a wet season (May–October, ~70% of the 1,000–1,700 mm annual rainfall) and a dry season (November–April). Single-campaign measurements at Marco Island, Rookery Bay and an SE-1 scrub ecotone site provided additional spatial context. Site coordinates, class, salinity and canopy metrics are in Supplementary Table 1.

### Seasonal campaigns and multi-scale framework

Repeat measurements spanned two campaigns: October 2022 (late wet season, inundated soils, elevated water) and March 2023 (dry season, lower water). Both included chamber fluxes, terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) and environmental sampling at the five core sites. Airborne eddy-covariance flights (CARAFE) were part of the broader NASA BlueFlux campaign — four deployments (April 2022, October 2022, February 2023, April 2023), of which October 2022 and February/March 2023 overlapped our ground campaigns. The design integrates four nested scales: static chambers isolating component flux densities; TLS-derived component surface areas that convert those densities to stand-level budgets; a continuous eddy-covariance tower at SRS-6; and CARAFE airborne fluxes across the full gradient. Bottom-up budgets (chambers × TLS) are evaluated against the top-down tower and airborne constraints. Full campaign context is given in ref. [BlueFlux ERL].

### Component chamber flux measurements

CH4 and CO2 fluxes were measured from individual components with closed dynamic chambers connected to portable analyzers (three ABB/LGR GLA131 microportable greenhouse-gas analyzers, off-axis ICOS; one Picarro G4301 cavity ring-down analyzer) logging dry-mole fractions at 1 Hz (LGR; 0.1 Hz in March 2022) or ~0.2 Hz (Picarro, which measures CH4 and CO2 on alternate logged rows; only fresh readings of each gas were used). Chamber designs were adapted to component geometry (Supplementary Methods; Supplementary Fig. 3): four elliptical stem-chamber sizes (enclosed areas 40–462 cm2) sealed to bark with modelling clay at 0, 50 and 100 cm above the sediment (and 150 cm where diameter allowed) to resolve vertical gradients diagnostic of soil-origin transport; prop-root chambers on individual *R. mangle* aerial roots; a transparent leaf chamber enclosing a branch cluster (used to determine flux direction and approximate magnitude, not a controlled physiological rate); open-bottom acrylic soil cylinders (23.5 cm diameter, inserted 30–91 cm) in the wet season and smaller PVC soil collars (14.3 or 19.4 cm) in the dry season; and floating chambers (19.4 cm) on surface water. Soil measurements included pneumatophores within the footprint, with density recorded. Chambers used closed-loop tubing with inline desiccant, were leak-tested before each measurement, and had system volumes computed for each chamber–analyzer combination (0.8–39.6 L). Measurements covered all four species, including standing dead trunks and snags at ghost sites (≥5 stems per species per site; 10 soil collars per plot). Fit windows had median lengths of 180–210 s by component; floating-chamber placements had a median of ~6.5 min (up to 79 min). In total 752 fluxes were analysed across six components: stems (n = 476), soil including pneumatophores (n = 118), water (n = 47 floating-chamber placements), roots (n = 65), coarse woody debris (n = 27) and leaves (n = 19).

### Flux calculation and ebullition partitioning

Fluxes were computed with the goFlux R package, fitting a linear model and the Hutchinson–Mosier nonlinear model to each concentration time series and reporting the flux selected by the goFlux best.flux criteria (the nonlinear model was considered only with ≥30 points, otherwise linear). Rates were derived from the initial slope, corrected for chamber volume, area, air temperature and barometric pressure, with air temperature and pressure from the co-located US-Skr tower at each measurement time (101.325 kPa where the tower had no pressure record, March 2022; Supplementary Methods S.M4). Minimum detectable fluxes were computed per measurement as 1.96 σ / t × the flux term, with σ the median absolute deviation of within-window first differences, centred per closure and pooled by analyzer and campaign; below-detection fluxes were retained at their measured values. Measurements with analyzer artefacts were excluded. Floating-chamber placements (chamber on to lift) were identified in the analyzer record, giving one flux per placement. Ebullition was separated with goAquaFlux (goFlux; de-ebulliated diffusive window): the diffusive flux was fitted on the de-ebulliated series (first 10 min for placements > 12 min) and ebullition was the summed bubble steps over the whole placement. For the Picarro, whose ~5 s readings do not resolve bubbles, the total is the two-point flux over the placement (Supplementary Fig. 1).

### Terrestrial laser scanning and surface-area scaling

Three-dimensional forest structure was measured with a RIEGL VZ-400i TLS system and GNSS positioning; point clouds were registered, georeferenced, and segmented into component classes (stems, prop roots, pneumatophores, ground surface), from which component surface area was extracted by class and height interval and scaled to the plot with species allometry. Full acquisition, registration, segmentation and allometry are in Supplementary Methods (S.M6). TLS-derived areas per unit ground area (m2 component surface m−2 ground) provided the scaling factors for both the CH4 and CO2 budgets.

### Bottom-up methane budgets

Stand-level CH4 budgets were constructed by multiplying component flux densities (nmol m−2 surface s−1) by the corresponding TLS surface areas per unit ground area, summed over components (soil, surface water, prop roots, stems, coarse woody debris). Species-specific stem rates were weighted by relative surface-area contribution. Because stem flux declines with height, stems were integrated over height using measured 0/50/100(/150) cm rates; the primary budget used an exponential-decay extrapolation above the highest measurement, with a zero-above-maximum alternative as a bound (Supplementary Text S.T1). Inundation state controls which surfaces emit and was set from measured chamber water depth: ghost sites (CP40, FLM30) were inundated in both analysed campaigns and emitted through the water surface, and tidal intact sites (SRS5, SRS6) were represented by high- and low-tide states weighted by the measured fraction of time the forest floor was flooded in each campaign month (FCE LTER water level above the soil surface), with prop-root and downed-wood surface below the water excluded at high tide. Wet-season water-surface fluxes at the intact sites, where no floating-chamber record exists, were estimated from dissolved CH4 and CO2 and a gas-transfer velocity calibrated on paired chamber and dissolved-gas measurements (k600 median 1.1 cm h⁻¹). Downed coarse woody debris is not resolved by TLS; its surface area was derived from the regional downed-wood volume of Krauss et al. (2005) as in Troxler et al. (2015) (Supplementary Text S.T3). The ghost budget is insensitive to whether the dry-season surface is treated as water or exposed soil (Supplementary Text S.T2). Uncertainties were propagated by Monte Carlo (below).

### Bottom-up CO2 net ecosystem exchange

Net ecosystem CO2 exchange was constructed as NEE = Reco − GPP (atmospheric sign; positive = source). Ecosystem respiration was built bottom-up by scaling chamber CO2 efflux densities with the same TLS surface areas (soil, water, prop roots, woody stems and branches) and adding a literature-based canopy foliar-respiration term. The foliar term used a species-weighted leaf dark respiration at 25 °C (Rd25; central 1.55, range 1.28–1.62 µmol m−2 s−1, from *R. mangle* and *A. germinans* at-site measurements), a short-term temperature response (Heskel et al. 2016; f(T) = exp[0.1012(T−25) − 0.0005(T2−252)]) driven by tower air temperature, a 30% daytime light-inhibition factor, and a Beer's-law canopy integration using an effective leaf-area index, LAIeff = (1 − e−kL)/k with k = 0.5 and L = 2.8 (range 2.3–5.55). GPP was not constructed bottom-up: it was taken from the co-located SRS-6 (AmeriFlux US-Skr) tower, where GPP is partitioned from observed NEE with a nighttime NEE–temperature respiration model (24-h campaign-window mean intact GPP ≈7.8 µmol m−2 s−1; Supplementary Methods S.M10). Ghost-forest GPP was assumed negligible (defoliated canopy), so ghost NEE ≈ Reco,bottom-up. Because imported tower GPP embeds the tower's own NEE and partitioned respiration, intact-forest bottom-up NEE reduces to NEE = NEEtower + (Reco,bottom-up − Reco,tower) and is treated as a reconciliation of bottom-up against tower-partitioned respiration rather than an independent net flux (Supplementary Text S.T5). Chamber respiration of stems, roots, soil and downed wood, measured near midday, was scaled to 24 h with the tower's within-month night-respiration Q10 (1.15; Supplementary Table 9). The leaf-respiration synthesis and provenance are in Supplementary Methods (S.M10) and Supplementary Table 6.

### Eddy-covariance tower and airborne fluxes

Continuous half-hourly CO2 and CH4 fluxes were measured at the SRS-6 tower (Florida Coastal Everglades LTER / AmeriFlux US-Skr) with standard eddy-covariance processing and friction-velocity filtering (Supplementary Methods S.M8). Landscape fluxes came from the CARAFE airborne eddy-covariance platform, with fluxes computed by continuous wavelet transform, footprint-weighted, and disaggregated to vegetation classes. Because the regenerating class was not spatially separable from the airborne footprints, closure used a two-end-member (intact, ghost) disaggregation following Delaria et al. (2024); the March 2023 airborne value is the mean of the February and April 2023 deployments. Chamber CH4 was compared on a nmol m−2 s−1 basis and chamber CO2 on a daily NEE basis after converting midday airborne fluxes to daily values. Full airborne instrumentation and processing are in Supplementary Methods (S.M9) and ref. [CARAFE JGR].

### Porewater geochemistry

Porewater was collected at four sites (SRS5, SRS6, BL60, CP40) at five depths (surface to 90 cm) with MHE PushPoint samplers during the dry-season campaign. Temperature, pH, conductivity, dissolved oxygen and oxidation–reduction potential were measured in the field (Hanna HI98494); sulfide and ferrous iron colorimetrically (Hach DR900). Dissolved CH4 and CO2 and their stable carbon isotopes were measured by headspace equilibration on a Picarro G2201-i; dissolved organic carbon, major ions and total alkalinity at the Yale Analytical and Stable Isotope Center (Supplementary Methods S.M11).

### Net radiative forcing and landscape scaling

Component CH4 fluxes were converted to CO2 equivalents using AR6 global warming potentials (GWP100 = 27.9, GWP20 = 81.2) and combined with net CO2 exchange to give net forcing (g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1) for each class. The disturbance-induced switch is the intact-to-ghost differential. A regional estimate multiplies the per-area differential by the area of Caribbean mangrove converted to ghost forest by recent hurricanes (Fig. 6e,f; extent source in Supplementary Methods S.M12).

### Statistical analysis

Fluxes were transformed with the inverse hyperbolic sine (asinh) before modelling. Component summaries and 95% confidence intervals used nonparametric bootstrap resampling (5,000 iterations, percentile method) stratified by component, site, season and class. Stem species and height effects were fitted with linear mixed-effects models (lme4/lmerTest, site random intercept), including a species × height-category interaction and alive/dead status, restricted to sites with species-level identification; estimated marginal means were computed on the asinh scale, back-transformed, and compared with Tukey-adjusted contrasts and Type III F-tests (Supplementary Table 3). Porewater structure was assessed by principal-components analysis of 11 centred, scaled variables. Budget and forcing uncertainties were propagated by Monte Carlo (5,000 draws) combining bootstrapped chamber flux densities, the leaf-respiration term (Rd25 ~ U[1.28, 1.62]; LAI lognormal with median 2.8 and 5.55 at the upper 97.5%; daytime light inhibition 0.2–0.5), the downed-wood area, tower GPP uncertainty and the CH4 budget, yielding 95% intervals for stand-level budgets and net forcing (Supplementary Text S.T4). Analyses were run in R (≥4.3); the full workflow is available at [repository URL].
