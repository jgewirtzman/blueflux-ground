**One-sentence summary.** Hurricane-killed mangroves become methane-amplified carbon sources, a climate feedback missing from blue-carbon accounting.

---

## Abstract

Saline mangroves are assumed to emit little methane, and blue-carbon accounting treats them as strong sinks. We measured methane and CO2 from six surfaces across intact, regenerating and hurricane-killed ("ghost") mangrove in the Florida Everglades, scaled them with terrestrial laser scanning, and checked them against tower and airborne eddy covariance. Intact forest emitted methane well above emission-factor assumptions, mostly from soils and prop roots. Mortality raised emission eightfold, moved it to ponded water, and reversed the porewater salinity–methane relationship. Ghost forest was a net source, a switch of ~6,400 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1 over 20 years, a third of ghost-forest warming coming from methane. Across Caribbean mangrove lost in 2017, the switch adds ~1.1 Tg CO2-eq yr−1, a feedback missing from current accounting.

## Main text

Mangrove forests cover ~147,000 km2 of tropical and subtropical coastline {bunting2022}, store 2–10 Pg of carbon {rovai2018;kauffman2020} and bury 18–23 Tg C yr−1 in their soils {breithaupt2022}. That capacity has made them central to blue-carbon policy, carbon crediting and nature-based climate solutions {macreadie2021;macreadie2019}. Like all wetlands, mangroves also produce methane, which warms the climate 81 times more than CO2 over 20 years and 28 times more over 100 {forster2021}. Methane may offset 17–23% of global mangrove soil carbon burial {rosentreter2018}, and a recent global estimate attributes a further ~17% to tree stems {qin2025}.

The prevailing view is that saline mangroves emit little methane. Tidal exchange delivers sulfate, and sulfate-reducing bacteria outcompete methanogens for shared substrates {burdige2012;martens1974;weston2006}; oxygen from tides and roots, and anaerobic oxidation coupled to sulfate reduction, consume much of the methane that is produced {cotovicz2024}. Methane emission therefore declines with salinity {bartlett1987;poffenbarger2011;alhaj2020}, offsets to carbon burial are far smaller in saline than in freshwater mangrove soils (~7% vs ~27%) {cotovicz2024}, and the IPCC 2013 Wetlands Supplement sets mangrove methane to zero above 18 PSU {ipcc2014}. Blue-carbon crediting methodologies assign only small default soil emission factors at these salinities (0.56–1.1 g CH4 m−2 yr−1 above 18–20 ppt) {vm0033}.

Two lines of evidence challenge that view. First, the framework rests mainly on soil measurements, yet methane also leaves wetlands through tidal water, prop roots, stems and dead wood {jeffrey2019;rosentreter2021;call2015;pangala2017;barba2019;qin2025}, and some woody surfaces even take methane up {gauci2024}. Component-resolved methane budgets for mangroves are almost absent from the Western Hemisphere, and none has been checked against an independent ecosystem-scale measurement. Second, methanogens that use substrates sulfate reducers do not compete for, such as methylamines and methanol, can persist in sulfate-rich and even hypersaline sediment {oremland1982;king1984;zhuang2016;jameson2019;narrowe2019;zhang2020}, and methanogenesis even proceeds in oxygenated wetland soils {angle2017}, so salinity need not suppress methane.

Mangroves are also increasingly killed. Tropical cyclones, sea-level rise and drought have caused widespread mangrove mortality {sippo2018;krauss2020;saintilan2023}. Cyclones defoliate canopies, kill root systems and pond water behind fallen wood, leaving standing "ghost forests" that can persist for years {smith2009;lagomasino2021;radabaugh2020;kirwan2019}, even where neighbouring stands resist and recover {riveramonroy2019}. Loss of root oxygen transport, a pulse of dead biomass and persistent flooding should all favour methane. Limited observations agree: creek methane rose 45% after a dieback in the Gulf of Mexico {yu2023}, and dead mangrove stems emitted eight times more methane than living ones in Australia {jeffrey2019}. But no study has resolved how mortality changes each emission pathway, checked those changes against an ecosystem-scale measurement, or asked whether mortality changes the net radiative balance. With the proportion of intense cyclones rising {ar6ch11}, this is a question about a growing climate feedback.

As part of NASA's BlueFlux campaign {poulter2023;doughty2026}, we measured methane and CO2 across a hurricane-disturbance gradient in the Florida coastal Everglades, where Hurricane Irma (September 2017) left extensive ghost forest {lagomasino2021} (Fig. 1). In Landsat greenness, our ghost stands lost their canopy in Irma and have not recovered, whereas the regenerating stand, equally defoliated, regrew to pre-storm greenness within five years (Fig. 1C). We combined chambers on six surfaces (water, soil, prop roots, stems, downed wood and leaves) at intact (Gunboat Island, SRS5; Lower Shark River, SRS6), regenerating (Bear Lake, BL60) and ghost (Christian Point, CP40; Flamingo, FLM30) sites in wet- and dry-season campaigns; terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) {calders2015;powell_tls} to measure the area of each surface; the US-Skr eddy-covariance tower; CARAFE airborne eddy covariance {carafe_instrument;delaria2024}, which resolves intact and ghost mangrove fluxes across south Florida; and porewater chemistry and sediment metagenomes to identify mechanisms (Fig. 1D).

### Mangroves emit methane from nearly every surface

Across 752 chamber fluxes, every surface except leaves emitted methane in every forest class (Fig. 2A). Water and soil had the highest rates (17.6 and 17.1 nmol m−2 s−1 across sites), followed by stems (8.0) and prop roots (3.5); downed wood was lower (1.0) and leaves near zero. More than 90% of stem, root and water fluxes were positive. Mortality raised per-area emission sharply. Relative to intact forest, ghost-forest stems emitted ~40 times more methane, water surfaces ~18 times more and soils twice as much, and the regenerating site had the highest soil and water rates of all (Fig. 2A). Emission was strongly seasonal: wet-season stem and water fluxes were four and nine times their dry-season values, tracking inundation, and bubbles supplied up to a third of water-surface methane at disturbed sites in the wet season (fig. S6). CO2 efflux from stems, roots, soils and water was positive in all classes, and leaves were the only net CO2 sink (Fig. 2B).

Woody emission was organized by height. Methane from stems and prop roots peaked at the water line in every class and declined upward (generalized additive model, p < 0.001 in each class; Fig. 2D), the signature of sediment-derived gas venting through the lower bole rather than production within the wood. Stem CO2 showed no such gradient (Fig. 2E). The water-line peak was 13–28 times higher in disturbed than intact forest, and the classes converged by ~1 m. Once height and class were accounted for, species, season and whether a stem was alive or dead had little effect, and dead stems kept venting methane. Apparent species differences at the stem base reflected where bark meets the water: white mangrove and buttonwood trunks reach the water surface, whereas red mangrove meets it through its prop roots.

High per-area rates did not translate into large stand-level fluxes. TLS showed that intact forest carries ~5.0 m2 of surface per m2 of ground, of which prop roots, stems and branches make up about a quarter, while ghost forest retains ~1.3 m2 m−2, mostly open water (Fig. 2G). Most woody surface lies far above the water line, where flux is lowest (Fig. 2F). Stems therefore contributed <1% of stand methane in both intact and ghost forest, whereas the dense prop-root zone of intact red-mangrove forest contributed ~28% (Fig. 2H). This bears directly on the recent global estimate that mangrove stems emit ~730 Gg CH4 yr−1 {qin2025}, which has been questioned for leaning on a few extreme values and on soil flux as a predictor {maher_preprint}. Scaled by measured bark area, intact Everglades stems emitted ~0.01 g CH4 m−2 yr−1, several hundred times less than that model predicts per unit ground area for mangroves in Mexico and Brazil (~7 g CH4 m−2 yr−1). Stem flux must be scaled by measured bark area and its height profile, not inferred from soil flux.

### Dead forests emit eight times more

Scaling each surface's flux by its area gives stand budgets (Fig. 3). Intact forest emitted 1.5 g CH4 m−2 yr−1 (2.5 at the most productive site), mostly from soils (54%) and prop roots (28%), with tidal water 16% (Fig. 2H). That is far above the zero that Tier-1 factors assign at these salinities {ipcc2014} and above the crediting default for porewater above 20 ppt {vm0033}, so wetland methane budgets omit an intact saline-mangrove source. Measured against local carbon burial (123 g C m−2 yr−1), all intact pathways together offset ~9% of burial in CO2 equivalents (GWP100), within the 7–23% range reported for mangrove soils and waters {rosentreter2018;cotovicz2024}, but well below the halving of net cooling reported for some subtropical estuarine mangroves {liu2020}. Ghost forest emitted 11.9 g CH4 m−2 yr−1, eight times more, ~87% of it through flooded water surfaces (Figs. 2H and 3B). Mortality thus moved emission from root-permeated sediment that drains on each tide to permanently ponded water. Respiration shifted in the same way: leaves dominated intact-forest respiration, whereas ghost-forest respiration came from water, sediment and standing and downed dead wood (Fig. 2I). The budgets were insensitive to how stem flux was extrapolated above measured heights (≤4%), because stems are a small term, and ghost-forest emission rests mainly on directly measured water fluxes scaled by inundated area (fig. S9).

The disturbance response was not simply monotonic. Assigning the regenerating stand woody and root areas between those of intact and ghost forest, at its measured inundation, gives ~33 g CH4 m−2 yr−1, more than ghost forest, suggesting that early regeneration may be the peak of the methane response rather than a return toward the intact baseline (Supplementary Text S6). A single dry-season visit to a ghost forest of different origin, a decades-old die-off behind a road that restricted tidal exchange, found little standing water and soil emission an order of magnitude below the Everglades ghost sites in the same season (Supplementary Text S8), consistent with drainage rather than ponding setting the methane response.

Our ground sites are intensive process sites: few in number, chosen to resolve where and why emissions change, not to represent the region by themselves. Two lines of evidence support them. Sites within a class agreed with each other (intact SRS5 and SRS6; ghost CP40 and FLM30; table S3), and they agreed with independent measurements at larger scale. Airborne eddy covariance, disaggregated to intact and ghost forest {delaria2024;hutjes2010;hannun2020}, agreed with the chamber budgets within uncertainty in both seasons. It ranked ghost above intact methane in the wet season (airborne 51 ± 27 vs 29 ± 16 nmol m−2 s−1; chambers 36 vs 4) and matched net CO2 exchange in sign and magnitude (Fig. 3, A to C). Bottom-up intact CO2 uptake (−1,025 g C m−2 yr−1) matched the eddy-covariance tower over the same forest (−1,288 over the campaign months; −1,170 ± 127 in the long-term record {barr2010}) (Fig. 3C). Chambers scaled by laser-scanned area and footprint-disaggregated airborne fluxes share no measurements, so their agreement does not depend on any one method's assumptions. The airborne fluxes integrate intact and ghost mangrove along flight legs across south Florida (fig. S20), so the agreement also shows that our sites are representative of their classes; the regional and climate-scale claims below rest on the class contrast that the airborne data resolve across the region, with the ground sites explaining its mechanism. _[PLACEHOLDER — airborne coverage: flight-leg footprint area over intact and ghost mangrove (km2) and the number of legs per class (E. ### A regime shift and a redox ladder flip

Porewater chemistry separated the forest classes (Fig. 4). The first principal component (43% of variance) contrasted salty, sulfate-rich, reduced, high-methane and high-ammonium ghost-forest porewater with oxidized intact-forest porewater. The second (24%) picked out the high dissolved organic carbon and alkalinity of the regenerating site (Fig. 4A). Ammonium at the ghost site (200–320 μM) exceeded 24 years of monitoring at the intact sites by more than an order of magnitude, a level matched in that record only after Irma (table S11), consistent with decomposing roots and the loss of root uptake.

The relationship between salinity and methane reversed (Fig. 4B). Across three porewater rounds, methane fell with salinity in intact and regenerating forest (r = −0.48), as expected where sulfate reduction suppresses methanogenesis, but rose with salinity in ghost forest (r = 0.60, p = 0.03). Ghost-forest porewater methane swung from 4 to 62 μM between seasons at nearly constant salinity (50–63 PSU), which salinity cannot explain. Ghost-forest porewater held seawater-level sulfate (~30 mM) alongside the highest dissolved methane, and alkalinity of 11–33 mM, five to fifteen times seawater, showed that sulfate reduction remained active everywhere (Fig. 4C). Methane therefore accumulated alongside sulfate reduction, not after it, as expected if methanogens use substrates sulfate reducers do not compete for, such as methylamines from the breakdown of osmolytes in dead roots {oremland1982;king1984;jones2019;krause2021}. _[PLACEHOLDER — metagenomes (Peccia lab): whether methylotrophic methanogens (mttB/mtbB/mtmB, Methanosarcinaceae) dominate the mcrA pool at the ghost site, normalized per gram of sediment and organic carbon, and whether methane oxidizers (pmoA) decline; Fig. 4, D to F.]_ Mortality thus inverts the redox expectation that governs methane in intact saline mangrove.

### A blue carbon sink becomes a source

Intact forest was a strong net sink. Its net CO2 uptake (−3,717 g CO2 m−2 yr−1) dwarfed its methane emission (+123 g CO2-eq at GWP20), which offset 3% of it (Fig. 5A). Ghost forest was a net source (+2,776 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1 at GWP20; +2,142 at GWP100). Losing the canopy turned its CO2 balance positive, and methane supplied 35% of its warming at GWP20 (16% at GWP100; 42% with the flow-based GWP* {smith2021}). Mortality therefore switched the radiative balance by ~6,400 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1 at GWP20 (~5,800 at GWP100). Most of that switch is lost CO2 uptake; methane is what turns the lost sink into a source. Monte Carlo intervals for the two states did not overlap (intact −5,183 to −1,854; ghost +1,931 to +3,977 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1).

The switch is robust to how the intact sink is defined. Mangroves export part of their fixed carbon to tidal waters {santos2021;ho2017}, so the carbon an intact forest retains is smaller than its net CO2 uptake. Counting exported alkalinity as a durable ocean store {sippo2016;reithmaier2023} and the rest as returned to the atmosphere, using a Shark River synthesis of lateral export {zhao2021}, the intact sink shrinks to ~−2,700 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1 and the switch remains ~5,400 (Fig. 3D; text S10). Across all other analytical choices we tested (flooding, temperature scaling, downed wood, canopy respiration and the tidal and daily timing of methane), the switch stayed between ~5,600 and ~8,000 (table S10).

Scaled across the Caribbean, the switch is large. In a Landsat map of 2017 hurricane damage {taillie2020}, 173 km2 of mangrove showed little recovery, 95% of it after Irma, mainly in northern Cuba and south Florida (Fig. 5C). At the measured per-area switch, this loss adds ~1.1 Tg CO2-eq yr−1 at GWP20 (0.7–1.6; 1.0 at GWP100), including 1.8 Gg CH4 yr−1 of induced methane (Fig. 5B). We treat this as a scenario: it assumes that hurricane dieback elsewhere in the region behaves like our ghost sites. No comparable measurements exist for hurricane-killed mangrove elsewhere. Die-offs from other causes suggest the response is general when stands stay wet: dead *Avicennia* forest behind a power-plant embankment in a Veracruz lagoon emitted up to ~16 nmol CH4 m−2 s−1 in the rainy season {romerouribe2022}, similar to our exposed ghost soils. Dead stands in Brazil emitted little after about three years {pacheco2024}, however, so how long the enhancement lasts is the main uncertainty. Our ghost sites showed no canopy recovery five to six years after landfall, so this forcing recurs each year until stands recover. The estimate is conservative. The map captures only 60 of the 108 km2 of post-Irma dieback in Florida {lagomasino2021}, and it omits peat collapse after root death {cahoon2003} and the lateral losses of ghost forest.

### From hurricanes to climate feedbacks

Two revisions follow for mangrove methane accounting: a non-zero baseline for intact saline forest, and an explicit dependence on disturbance state. Hypersaline ghost forest (50–64 PSU) emitted eight times more methane than intact forest, the opposite of what salinity thresholds predict {ipcc2014;vm0033}. Mortality also changes where methane escapes, from sediment and roots to ponded water, so emission factors built from soil chambers in intact forest will miss most of the disturbed-forest flux. The response spans a wide range across settings and may peak during regeneration, so disturbed mangroves need their own measured factors rather than a single multiplier.

Trees are part of this picture but not in the way recent global estimates suggest. Stems behaved as conduits rather than sources: emission peaked where bark meets the water and fell with height, consistent with sediment gas moving up through aerenchyma and lenticels {barba2019}, and it persisted in standing dead stems {martinez2021}, which lose active root-driven transport but keep a passive pathway and a decomposing source. At the water line, ghost-forest stems emitted 12.5 nmol m−2 s−1 against 0.9 in intact forest, close to the contrast between dead and live stem bases after climate-driven dieback in northern Australia (12.4 vs 1.1) {jeffrey2019}, the only comparable measurement we know of; yet stems stayed below 1% of both stand budgets because little bark sits near the water line. Per-area rates and stand contributions must therefore be kept distinct, and stem fluxes scaled by measured bark area and height rather than extrapolated from soil fluxes {qin2025;maher_preprint}.

Closing the budget in this way also identifies what is still missing. Ground replication is limited to two sites per class (one regenerating site), which is why we treat the ground sites as process sites and rest the regional claims on the airborne contrast. Agreement between chambers and aircraft was within uncertainty, not exact: intact-forest methane from the aircraft in the wet season exceeded the chamber budget, which samples near slack water and in daylight, so tidal pulses, night-time emission and lateral transport of dissolved methane are the most likely unmeasured terms. These are the priorities for closing mangrove methane budgets elsewhere, and the combination used here, component chambers scaled by measured structure and checked against airborne fluxes, offers a template for other blue-carbon and disturbed ecosystems.

Hurricane mortality is therefore a positive climate feedback that current accounting omits. The area we scaled reflects essentially one storm season in one region, a small fraction of the cyclone-exposed mangrove coast worldwide {mo2023;peereman2022}, and the proportion of intense cyclones is projected to rise {ar6ch11}. Because recovery restores the sink, the feedback strengthens the case for conserving intact mangrove and restoring damaged stands. Regeneration did not bring methane back down. The regenerating stand had recolonized naturally behind an impoundment and still emitted as much as ghost forest, so canopy recovery is not the same as restoring the hydrology. Whether restoring tidal exchange to impounded die-off {lewis2005} lowers methane, and how long the enhancement lasts after mortality, are open questions that before–after studies of restoration could answer. Blue-carbon crediting should carry both an accurate methane baseline and a permanence-risk term for disturbance {rosentreter2021gbc;williamson2022}.

Our conclusions are bounded by a space-for-time gradient, two chamber campaigns that miss diurnal, tidal and summer variation, literature-based lateral export and burial, and a regenerating class without TLS or airborne closure (supplementary text). None changes the direction or robustness of the switch. Measuring every pathway, scaling by measured structure and checking against airborne fluxes shows that a hurricane-killed mangrove is not a diminished sink but a methane-amplified source.
