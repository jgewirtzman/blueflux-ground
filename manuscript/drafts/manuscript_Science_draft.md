**One-sentence summary.** Hurricane-killed mangroves become methane-amplified carbon sources, a climate feedback missing from blue-carbon accounting.

---

## Abstract

Saline mangroves are assumed to emit little methane, and blue-carbon accounting treats them as strong sinks. We measured methane and CO2 from six surfaces across intact, regenerating and hurricane-killed ("ghost") mangrove in the Florida Everglades, scaled them with terrestrial laser scanning, and checked them against tower and airborne fluxes. Intact forest emitted more methane than emission factors assume, mostly from soils and prop roots. Mortality raised emission eightfold, moved it to ponded water, and stripped the sediment's oxidizing layer, possibly blocking regrowth. Ghost forest was a net source, a switch of ~6,400 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1 over 20 years, a third of ghost-forest warming from methane. Across Caribbean mangrove lost in 2017, the switch adds ~1.1 Tg CO2-eq yr−1, a feedback current accounting omits.

## Main text

Mangrove forests cover ~147,000 km2 of tropical and subtropical coastline {bunting2022}, store 2–10 Pg of carbon {rovai2018;kauffman2020} and bury 18–23 Tg C yr−1 in their soils {breithaupt2022}. That capacity has made them central to blue-carbon policy, carbon crediting and nature-based climate solutions {macreadie2021;macreadie2019}. Like all wetlands, mangroves also produce methane, which warms the climate 81 times more than CO2 over 20 years and 28 times more over 100 {forster2021}. Methane may offset 17–23% of global mangrove soil carbon burial {rosentreter2018}, and a recent global estimate attributes a further ~17% to tree stems {qin2025}.

The prevailing view is that saline mangroves emit little methane. Tidal exchange delivers sulfate, and sulfate-reducing bacteria outcompete methanogens for shared substrates {burdige2012;martens1974;weston2006}; oxygen from tides and roots, and anaerobic oxidation coupled to sulfate reduction, consume much of the methane that is produced {cotovicz2024}. Methane emission therefore declines with salinity {bartlett1987;poffenbarger2011;alhaj2020}, offsets to carbon burial are far smaller in saline than in freshwater mangrove soils (~7% vs ~27%) {cotovicz2024}, and the IPCC 2013 Wetlands Supplement sets mangrove methane to zero above 18 PSU {ipcc2014}. Blue-carbon crediting methodologies assign only small default soil emission factors at these salinities (0.56–1.1 g CH4 m−2 yr−1 above 18–20 ppt) {vm0033}.

Two lines of evidence challenge that view. First, the framework rests mainly on soil measurements, yet methane also leaves wetlands through tidal water, prop roots, stems and dead wood {jeffrey2019;rosentreter2021;call2015;pangala2017;barba2019;qin2025}, and some woody surfaces even take methane up {gauci2024}. Component-resolved methane budgets for mangroves are almost absent from the Western Hemisphere, and none has been checked against an independent ecosystem-scale measurement. Second, methanogens that use substrates sulfate reducers do not compete for, such as methylamines and methanol, can persist in sulfate-rich and even hypersaline sediment {oremland1982;king1984;zhuang2016;jameson2019;narrowe2019;zhang2020}, and methanogenesis even proceeds in oxygenated wetland soils {angle2017}, so salinity need not suppress methane.

Mangroves are also increasingly killed. Tropical cyclones, sea-level rise and drought have caused widespread mangrove mortality {sippo2018;krauss2020;saintilan2023}. Cyclones defoliate canopies, kill root systems and pond water behind fallen wood, leaving standing "ghost forests" that can persist for years {smith2009;lagomasino2021;radabaugh2020;kirwan2019}, even where neighbouring stands resist and recover {riveramonroy2019}. Whether a stand regrows sets how long any change in its emissions lasts. Loss of root oxygen transport, a pulse of dead biomass and persistent flooding should all favour methane. Limited observations agree: creek methane rose 45% after a dieback in the Gulf of Mexico {yu2023}, and dead mangrove stems emitted eight times more methane than living ones in Australia {jeffrey2019}. But no study has resolved how mortality changes each emission pathway, checked those changes against an ecosystem-scale measurement, or asked whether mortality changes the net radiative balance. With the proportion of intense cyclones rising {ar6ch11}, this is a question about a growing climate feedback.

As part of the NASA Carbon Monitoring System BlueFlux campaign {poulter2023}, we measured methane and CO2 across a hurricane-disturbance gradient in the Florida coastal Everglades, where Hurricane Irma (September 2017) left extensive ghost forest {lagomasino2021} (Fig. 1). In Landsat greenness, our ghost stands lost their canopy in Irma and have not recovered, whereas the regenerating stand, equally defoliated, regrew to pre-storm greenness within five years (Fig. 1C). We combined chambers on six surfaces (water, soil, prop roots, stems, downed wood and leaves) at intact (Gunboat Island, SRS5; Lower Shark River, SRS6), regenerating (Bear Lake, BL60) and ghost (Christian Point, CP40; Flamingo, FLM30) sites in wet- and dry-season campaigns; terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) {calders2015;powell_tls} to measure the area of each surface; the US-Skr eddy-covariance tower; CARAFE airborne eddy covariance {carafe_instrument;delaria2024}, which resolves intact and ghost mangrove fluxes across south Florida; and porewater chemistry and sediment metagenomes to identify mechanisms (Fig. 1D; figs. S1 to S5).

### Mangroves emit methane from nearly every surface

Across 752 chamber fluxes, every surface except leaves emitted methane in every forest class (Fig. 2A; figs. S6 and S7). Water and soil had the highest rates (17.6 and 17.1 nmol m−2 s−1 across sites), followed by stems (8.0) and prop roots (3.5); downed wood was lower (1.0) and leaves near zero. More than 90% of stem, root and water fluxes were positive. Mortality raised per-area emission sharply. Relative to intact forest, ghost-forest stems emitted ~40 times more methane, water surfaces ~18 times more and soils twice as much, and the regenerating site had the highest soil and water rates of all (Fig. 2A). Emission was strongly seasonal: wet-season stem and water fluxes were four and nine times their dry-season values, tracking inundation, and bubbles supplied up to a third of water-surface methane at disturbed sites in the wet season (figs. S8 and S9). CO2 efflux from stems, roots, soils and water was positive in all classes, and leaves were the only net CO2 sink (Fig. 2B).

Woody emission was organized by height. Methane from stems and prop roots peaked at the water line in every class and declined upward (generalized additive model, p < 0.001 in each class; Fig. 2D), the signature of sediment-derived gas venting through the lower bole, through aerenchyma and lenticels, rather than production within the wood {barba2019}. Stem CO2 showed no such gradient (Fig. 2E). The water-line peak was 13–28 times higher in disturbed than intact forest, and the classes converged by ~1 m. Once height and class were accounted for, species, season and whether a stem was alive or dead had little effect (fig. S10), and dead stems kept venting methane {martinez2021}: losing active, root-driven transport left a passive pathway and a decomposing source. Apparent species differences at the stem base reflected where bark meets the water: white mangrove and buttonwood trunks reach the water surface, whereas red mangrove meets it through its prop roots.

High per-area rates did not translate into large stand-level fluxes. TLS showed that intact forest carries ~5.0 m2 of surface per m2 of ground, of which prop roots, stems and branches make up about a quarter, while ghost forest retains ~1.3 m2 m−2, mostly open water (Fig. 2G). Most woody surface lies far above the water line, where flux is lowest (Fig. 2F; figs. S11 and S12). At the water line, ghost-forest stems emitted 12.5 nmol m−2 s−1 against 0.9 in intact forest, close to the contrast between dead and live stem bases after climate-driven dieback in northern Australia (12.4 vs 1.1) {jeffrey2019}, the only comparable measurement we know of. Yet stems contributed <1% of stand methane in both intact and ghost forest, whereas the dense prop-root zone of intact red-mangrove forest contributed ~28% (Fig. 2H). This bears directly on the recent global estimate that mangrove stems emit ~730 Gg CH4 yr−1 {qin2025}, which has been questioned for leaning on a few extreme values and on soil flux as a predictor {maher_preprint}. Scaled by measured bark area, intact Everglades stems emitted ~0.01 g CH4 m−2 yr−1, several hundred times less than that model predicts per unit ground area for mangroves in Mexico and Brazil (~7 g CH4 m−2 yr−1). Per-area rates and stand contributions must be kept distinct: stem flux must be scaled by measured bark area and its height profile, not inferred from soil flux.

### Dead forests emit eight times more

Scaling each surface's flux by its area gives stand budgets (Fig. 3; figs. S13 to S15). Intact forest emitted 1.5 g CH4 m−2 yr−1 (2.5 at the most productive site), mostly from soils (54%) and prop roots (28%), with tidal water 16% (Fig. 2H). That is far above the zero that Tier-1 factors assign at these salinities {ipcc2014} and above the crediting default for porewater above 20 ppt {vm0033}, so wetland methane budgets omit an intact saline-mangrove source. Measured against local carbon burial (123 g C m−2 yr−1), all intact pathways together offset ~9% of burial in CO2 equivalents (GWP100), within the 7–23% range reported for mangrove soils and waters {rosentreter2018;cotovicz2024}, but well below the halving of net cooling reported for some subtropical estuarine mangroves {liu2020}. Ghost forest emitted 11.9 g CH4 m−2 yr−1, eight times more, ~87% of it through flooded water surfaces (Figs. 2H and 3B). Mortality thus moved emission from root-permeated sediment that drains on each tide to permanently ponded water. Respiration shifted in the same way: leaves dominated intact-forest respiration, whereas ghost-forest respiration came from water, sediment and standing and downed dead wood (Fig. 2I). The budgets were insensitive to how stem flux was extrapolated above measured heights (≤4%), because stems are a small term, and ghost-forest emission rests mainly on directly measured water fluxes scaled by inundated area (fig. S14).

### Independent checks at ecosystem scale

Our ground sites are intensive process sites: few in number, chosen to resolve where and why emissions change, not to represent the region by themselves. Sites within a class agreed with each other (intact SRS5 and SRS6; ghost CP40 and FLM30; table S1), and the budgets agreed with two independent measurements at larger scale. Bottom-up intact CO2 uptake (−1,025 g C m−2 yr−1) matched the eddy-covariance tower over the same forest (−1,288 over the campaign months; −1,170 ± 127 in the long-term record {barr2010}) (Fig. 3C). Airborne eddy covariance, disaggregated to intact and ghost mangrove along flight legs across south Florida {delaria2024;hutjes2010;hannun2020} (fig. S16), shares no measurements with the chambers, yet agreed with the chamber budgets within uncertainty in both seasons. It ranked ghost above intact methane in the wet season (airborne 51 ± 27 vs 29 ± 16 nmol m−2 s−1; chambers 36 vs 4) and matched intact-forest net CO2 exchange in sign and magnitude (Fig. 3, A to C). Over ghost forest the aircraft saw a small midday CO2 sink where the chamber budget, a sum of releases, shows a source; primary production the chambers capture only in part, with some signs of photosynthesis in the ponds and at the vegetated margins and possibly in algal mats, may account for the difference (text S1). The agreement indicates that the intact–ghost contrast measured at our sites holds across south Florida. _[PLACEHOLDER — airborne coverage: flight-leg footprint area over intact and ghost mangrove (km2) and the number of legs per class (E. ### Mortality removes the sediment's oxidizing layer

Porewater chemistry separated the forest classes (Fig. 4; figs. S17 to S19). The first principal component (39% of variance) contrasted salty, sulfate-rich, reduced, high-methane and high-ammonium ghost-forest porewater with oxidized intact-forest porewater; the second (24%) picked out the high dissolved organic carbon and alkalinity of the regenerating site (Fig. 4A). The profiles show what mortality removed. In intact forest the upper sediment stayed oxidized, most clearly at SRS6: in the top 0–45 cm dissolved O2 was present, redox potential was highest, sulfide low and dissolved iron present, and methane declined and became isotopically heavier toward the surface, consistent with oxidation (Fig. 4D; text S2). Two processes keep such a layer oxidized, and our data cannot separate them: oxygen leaking from living prop roots and pneumatophores {mckee1988;kristensen2006}, and tidal flushing, which at the intact sites raised and drained the water by 13–31 cm each day (Fig. 4C). Ghost forest had lost both. Its water ponded, with little tidal exchange (Fig. 4C), and its porewater was anoxic to the surface: sulfide reached 0.8 mM at the sediment surface with little iron to bind it, and methane peaked at the surface rather than at depth, with no isotopic front, so methane produced among dead roots and litter met no oxidizing barrier before reaching the ponded water. Ammonium (200–320 μM) exceeded 24 years of monitoring at the intact sites by more than an order of magnitude, a level matched in that record only after Irma (table S2), consistent with decomposing roots and the loss of root uptake.

Salinity no longer suppressed methane in ghost forest (Fig. 4B). Across three sampling rounds, allowing for the difference between porewater and surface water, methane fell with salinity in intact forest (r = −0.33, p = 0.07) and regenerating forest (r = −0.56, p = 0.001), as it did in the river and bay channels (r = −0.57) and as expected where sulfate reduction suppresses methanogenesis, but not in ghost forest (r = 0.16, p = 0.40), where porewater held the most methane at the highest salinities. Ghost-forest porewater methane swung from 5 to 75 μM between seasons at nearly constant salinity (59–64 PSU), which salinity cannot explain. Sulfate reduction remained active everywhere: ghost-forest porewater still held ~30 mM sulfate, near seawater concentration but, below the surface, 23–32% less than its salinity implies, and alkalinity of 11–33 mM, five to fifteen times seawater, accompanied sulfate loss at every site (Fig. 4D; text S2). Methane therefore accumulated alongside sulfate reduction, not after it, as expected if methanogens use substrates sulfate reducers do not compete for, such as methylamines from the breakdown of osmolytes in dead roots {oremland1982;king1984;jones2019;krause2021}. _[PLACEHOLDER — metagenomes (Peccia lab): whether methylotrophic methanogens (mttB/mtbB/mtmB, Methanosarcinaceae) dominate the mcrA pool at the ghost site, normalized per gram of sediment and organic carbon, and whether methane oxidizers (pmoA) decline; Fig. 4, E to G.]_ Mortality thus inverts the redox expectation that governs methane in intact saline mangrove.

### Why ghost forests persist

The same chemistry may explain why ghost forest persists. A seedling rooting at CP40 would meet two stresses. The first is salinity: porewater of 50–55 PSU, well above the range in which red-mangrove establishment declines {krauss2008}. The second is sulfide: ~0.8 mM in the top centimetres, about half of it as H2S at pH 6.7–6.9. Sulfide near 1–2 mM reduces photosynthesis and growth of red-mangrove seedlings {linsternberg1992} by inhibiting root respiration and nutrient uptake {koch1990;lamers2013}. Without living roots or tidal flushing to oxidize the surface sediment, and in carbonate sediments that hold too little iron to bind it {chambers2001;julian2017}, sulfide reaches the root zone {mckee1988;thibodeau1986}; a hydrologically isolated dead stand in Mexico reached similar conditions {perezceballos2022}. Sulfide accumulation was inferred for Irma's ponded dieback {lagomasino2021}; these profiles measure it six years on. The regenerating stand is the contrast. Impounded and rain-fed, it had fresh surface water (3 PSU), less saline porewater (33–35 PSU) and little sulfide at the sediment surface (0.08 mM), and its canopy recovered within five years (Fig. 1C). Sulfate reduction there was as intense as anywhere, and at every site most of the sulfide it produced was removed from solution (text S2), so what separates the stands is where sulfide remains, not how much is made. With one regenerating site this is a hypothesis, and in recovering Everglades stands that were not ponded, seedling growth tracked phosphorus and light rather than sulfide {restrepo2025}. But it suggests a feedback: in ponded, hypersaline basins, mortality creates sediment conditions that prevent regrowth, and so prolongs the emission it caused.

Hydrology appears to set how long the methane response lasts. The regenerating stand, recolonized behind an impoundment, emitted as much methane as ghost forest: assigning it woody and root areas between those of intact and ghost forest, at its measured inundation, gives ~33 g CH4 m−2 yr−1, more than ghost forest, so early regeneration may be the peak of the response rather than a return toward the intact baseline (text S3). By contrast, a decades-old die-off behind a road that restricted tidal exchange had little standing water in the dry season and soil emission an order of magnitude below the Everglades ghost sites (text S4), and dead stands in Brazil emitted little after about three years {pacheco2024}. Methane from dead mangrove therefore persists where water ponds and declines where it drains.

### A blue carbon sink becomes a source

Intact forest was a strong net sink. Its net CO2 uptake (−3,743 g CO2 m−2 yr−1) dwarfed its methane emission (+123 g CO2-eq at GWP20), which offset 3% of it (Fig. 5A). Ghost forest was a net source (+2,776 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1 at GWP20; +2,142 at GWP100). Losing the canopy turned its CO2 balance positive, and methane supplied 35% of its warming at GWP20 (16% at GWP100; 42% with the flow-based GWP* {smith2021}). Mortality therefore switched the radiative balance by ~6,400 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1 at GWP20 (~5,800 at GWP100). Most of that switch is lost CO2 uptake; methane is what turns the lost sink into a source. Monte Carlo intervals for the two states did not overlap (intact −5,183 to −1,854; ghost +1,931 to +3,977 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1).

The switch is robust to how the intact sink is defined. Mangroves export part of their fixed carbon to tidal waters {santos2021;ho2017}, so the carbon an intact forest retains is smaller than its net CO2 uptake. Counting exported alkalinity as a durable ocean store {sippo2016;reithmaier2023} and the rest as returned to the atmosphere, using a Shark River synthesis of lateral export {zhao2021}, the intact sink shrinks to ~−2,700 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1 and the switch remains ~5,400 (Fig. 3D; text S5). Across all other analytical choices we tested (flooding, temperature scaling, downed wood, canopy respiration and the tidal and daily timing of methane), the switch stayed between ~5,600 and ~8,000 (table S3; fig. S20). Independent routes agree in sign and order of magnitude. The airborne class fluxes alone, with midday CO2 scaled to daily by the regional tower relation {doughty2026}, give a switch of ~4,500 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1 at GWP20 (2,000–7,000), and the BlueFlux regional upscaling over all mapped 2017 dieback gives ~1,700 {doughty2026}. Both are smaller, as expected for estimates that average over flight footprints or 500 m pixels and so mix persistent ghost forest with regreening stands and live margins, which our process sites separate (text S1; fig. S21).

Scaled across the Caribbean, the switch is large. In a Landsat map of 2017 hurricane damage {taillie2020}, 173 km2 of mangrove showed little recovery, 95% of it after Irma, mainly in northern Cuba and south Florida (Fig. 5C). At the measured per-area switch, this loss adds ~1.1 Tg CO2-eq yr−1 at GWP20 (0.7–1.6; 1.0 at GWP100), including 1.8 Gg CH4 yr−1 of induced methane (Fig. 5B). We treat this as a scenario: it assumes that hurricane dieback elsewhere in the region behaves like our ghost sites. No comparable measurements exist for hurricane-killed mangrove elsewhere. Die-offs from other causes suggest the response is general when stands stay wet: dead *Avicennia* forest behind a power-plant embankment in a Veracruz lagoon emitted up to ~16 nmol CH4 m−2 s−1 in the rainy season {romerouribe2022}, similar to our exposed ghost soils. How long the enhancement lasts is the main uncertainty; at our sites, with no canopy recovery five to six years after landfall and sediment that may prevent it, the forcing recurs each year until stands recover. The estimate is conservative. The map captures only 60 of the 108 km2 of post-Irma dieback in Florida {lagomasino2021}, and it omits peat collapse after root death {cahoon2003} and the lateral losses of ghost forest.

### From hurricanes to climate feedbacks

Two revisions follow for mangrove methane accounting: a non-zero baseline for intact saline forest, and an explicit dependence on disturbance state. Hypersaline ghost forest (50–64 PSU) emitted eight times more methane than intact forest, the opposite of what salinity thresholds predict {ipcc2014;vm0033}. Mortality also changes where methane escapes, from sediment and roots to ponded water, so emission factors built from soil chambers in intact forest will miss most of the disturbed-forest flux. The response spans a wide range across settings and may peak during regeneration, so disturbed mangroves need their own measured factors rather than a single multiplier.

These conclusions rest on a space-for-time comparison with limited replication: two ground sites per class (one regenerating site, without TLS or airborne closure) and two chamber campaigns that miss night, summer and most of the tidal cycle. We therefore treated the ground sites as process sites and tested them against airborne class fluxes before scaling. Chambers and aircraft agreed within uncertainty but not exactly. In the wet season the aircraft saw more intact-forest methane than the chamber budget, which samples near slack water and in daylight, so tidal pulses, night-time emission and lateral transport of dissolved methane are the likeliest missing terms; lateral export and burial come from the literature (supplementary text). None of these changes the direction of the switch, which held across every analytical choice we tested (table S3). They mark what to measure next, and the approach used here (chambers on every surface, scaled by measured structure and checked against airborne fluxes) is a template for other blue-carbon and disturbed ecosystems.

Hurricane mortality is therefore a positive climate feedback that current accounting omits. The dieback we scaled reflects essentially one storm season in one region, a small fraction of the cyclone-exposed mangrove coast {mo2023;peereman2022}, and the proportion of intense cyclones is projected to rise {ar6ch11}. Recovery restores the sink, which strengthens the case for conserving intact mangrove and restoring damaged stands, but regrowth alone was not enough: the regenerating stand, recolonized behind an impoundment, still emitted as much methane as ghost forest. Restoring canopy is not restoring hydrology. Reconnecting tidal flushing could act on both problems at once, draining ponded water and removing the sulfide and salt that may block regrowth; whether reopening tidal exchange to impounded die-off {lewis2005} brings methane down, and how long the enhancement lasts, are questions that before–after studies of restoration can answer. Blue-carbon crediting should carry both an accurate methane baseline and a permanence-risk term for disturbance {rosentreter2021gbc;williamson2022}. A hurricane-killed mangrove is not a diminished sink; it is a methane-amplified source.
