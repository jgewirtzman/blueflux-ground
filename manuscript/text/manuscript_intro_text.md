# Introduction

Mangrove forests provide coastal protection, support fisheries, filter nutrients, and harbor biodiversity across approximately 147,000 km2 of tropical and subtropical coastlines. Among their most globally significant functions is carbon sequestration: mangrove soils store 2-10 Pg C and accumulate 18-23 Tg C yr-1, and lateral export of dissolved and particulate carbon to the coastal ocean represents a further major flux (118 Tg C yr-1). This capacity has made mangroves central to blue carbon policy, carbon crediting, and nature-based climate solutions. However, like all wetlands, mangroves produce methane (CH4), whose global warming potential is 28-80 times that of CO2. Whether CH4 emissions substantially offset mangrove carbon burial --- and under what conditions --- remains a critical uncertainty for climate accounting.

The prevailing understanding is that high-salinity mangrove soils produce little CH4. Net CH4 emission is the balance of production and consumption, and in saline systems both sides of this balance disfavor emission. On the production side, the thermodynamic redox ladder dictates that more energetically favorable electron acceptors --- oxygen, then nitrate, iron, and sulfate --- are used before methanogenesis can proceed; tidal flooding replenishes sulfate and oxygen, sustaining this competitive suppression. On the consumption side, aerobic methanotrophs oxidize CH4 near the soil-water interface, and anaerobic oxidation of methane (AOM) coupled to sulfate reduction may consume a substantial fraction of CH4 produced at depth, though AOM rates in mangroves remain poorly quantified. The net result is that CH4 offsets to carbon burial are much higher in freshwater wetland soils (~27%), where methanogenesis dominates organic matter mineralization, than in saline soils (~7%). This relationship underpins the IPCC 2013 Supplement for Wetlands Tier 1 framework, which assumes annual CH4 emissions are negligible above 18 PSU --- effectively zero for most mangrove forests worldwide. However, these fixed, biome-level emission factors do not account for ecosystem state. Emerging evidence challenges this framework: CH4 emissions have been documented from mangrove soils, surface waters, and tree stems across a range of salinities, with global estimates suggesting CH4 may offset 17-23% of mangrove soil carbon burial overall, and stem emissions alone ~17%. Methanogenesis has been observed under sulfate-rich and hypersaline conditions once considered prohibitive, and net CH4 flux reflects a balance between production and consumption by microbial communities whose metabolic flexibility exceeds what redox thermodynamics alone would predict. The magnitude and mechanisms of mangrove CH4 emissions vary with tree species, size, stem height, hydroperiod, and geomorphic setting, but component-resolved CH4 budgets --- partitioning emissions among stems, roots, soils, water, and woody debris --- are almost entirely absent from the Western Hemisphere.

Mangroves are simultaneously threatened by deforestation, sea-level rise, and tropical cyclones. Tropical cyclone intensity and the proportion of major hurricanes are increasing under climate change. Cyclones defoliate canopy, kill root systems, compress soils, alter hydrology through ponding, and create standing dead wood. Loss of live root oxygen transport shifts redox conditions, decomposition of killed biomass supplies labile carbon to methanogens, and new emission surfaces emerge from dead wood and expanded inundation. Limited observations support this expectation: CH4 emissions from mangrove creeks increased 45% after dieback in the Gulf of Mexico, and dead stems emitted eight times more CH4 than living ones in Australia, though the generality of these effects across species remains uncertain. No study has quantified how hurricane disturbance alters component-specific CH4 pathways, validated those changes against ecosystem-scale observations, or tested whether the salinity-methanogenesis relationship persists in disturbed systems.

Resolving this requires measurements at multiple scales: static chambers to isolate flux rates from individual components, but also ecosystem-scale constraints to validate the resulting budgets. Bottom-up estimates derived from chambers alone cannot capture the full emission landscape, while top-down observations from towers and aircraft integrate all sources but cannot attribute them to specific pathways without independent component information. Here, as part of the NASA BlueFlux campaign, we investigate CH4 offsets to mangrove carbon accrual and how fluxes, pathways, and biogeochemistry change with hurricane disturbance in the Florida coastal Everglades, where Hurricane Irma (2017) caused extensive mangrove mortality. We combine static chambers on seven ecosystem components, terrestrial laser scanning for surface area quantification, eddy covariance towers, and the CARAFE airborne eddy covariance instrument across a gradient from intact forest to ghost forest. We construct the first component-resolved CH4 budgets for hurricane-disturbed mangroves, compare bottom-up estimates to top-down constraints, and assess whether hurricane-driven mortality switches these ecosystems from net radiative cooling to net warming.
