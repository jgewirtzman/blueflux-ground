# Results

## R.1 Airborne top-down fluxes across the disturbance gradient

Landscape-scale top-down CH4 and CO2 fluxes for the two mangrove end-members -- intact mangrove forest and hurricane-killed ghost forest -- are drawn from the NASA BlueFlux airborne eddy-covariance campaigns, disaggregated by vegetation class via footprint analysis and multilinear regression (Delaria et al. 2024). We adopt this two end-member (intact, ghost) representation because a distinct regenerating-mangrove class could not be reliably separated in the airborne footprint disaggregation, and because terrestrial laser scanning for bottom-up scaling was available only at the two end-members. Because the top-down fluxes serve here as the independent constraint on the bottom-up budgets, they are presented together with the closure comparison in Sect. R.5 (Fig. 8).

## R.2 Component-specific flux rates and pathway partitioning

We measured 867 CH4 and CO2 fluxes from seven ecosystem components across eight mangrove sites spanning a hurricane disturbance gradient, during dry (March 2022), wet (October 2022), and dry (March 2023) seasonal campaigns (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Soils showed the highest mean CH4 flux rates (24.7 nmol m-2 s-1, 95% CI: 14.8-37.0, n = 118), followed by water surfaces (15.1, CI: 10.0-21.0, n = 92), stems (10.2, CI: 6.0-16.0, n = 498), and roots (3.8, CI: 1.1-8.7, n = 65). Coarse woody debris emitted at a lower rate (1.1, CI: 0.1-2.7, n = 27), while leaf fluxes were near zero (0.02, CI: 0.01-0.03, n = 19). 92.4% of stem and 92.3% of root CH4 fluxes were positive (net emission to the atmosphere); 100% of water surface measurements were positive. Pneumatophore density was not significantly correlated with soil CH4 flux at any site (Spearman rho = -0.23 to 0.64, all p > 0.08, n = 8-22 per site; Fig. S2a), though soil CO2 flux was positively correlated with pneumatophore density at SRS6 (rho = 0.79, p = 0.006; Fig. S2b).

CH4 fluxes were higher at disturbed than healthy sites across all components. Stem CH4 in ghost forest (11.5, CI: 7.2-17.0, n = 219) and regenerating forest (14.7, CI: 3.9-33.0, n = 101) exceeded healthy mangrove (0.5, CI: 0.2-0.9, n = 191) by 23x and 29x, respectively. Soil CH4 was 78.6 (CI: 38.3-126.8, n = 23) at regenerating, 22.0 (CI: 8.9-39.6, n = 31) at ghost, and 6.6 (CI: 3.2-11.9, n = 64) at healthy sites. Water surface CH4 was 34.7 (CI: 14.7-60.5, n = 9) at regenerating, 19.6 (CI: 12.3-28.1, n = 60) at ghost, and 0.7 (CI: 0.5-0.9, n = 18) at healthy sites.

Wet-season stem CH4 (19.8, CI: 9.5-32.9, n = 206) was 5.7x dry-season (3.5, CI: 2.2-4.9, n = 292). Wet-season water CH4 (40.8, CI: 27.9-55.1, n = 27) was 9.1x dry-season (4.5, CI: 3.4-5.8, n = 65). Ghost forest sites were entirely inundated during the wet season, with all measurements taken over water (n = 19) and none over exposed soil; in the dry season, lower water levels exposed soil surfaces (31 soil, 41 water measurements). Of 75 water-surface flux measurements, ebullition occurred exclusively in the wet season (6 of 23 measurements, 26%; Fig. S1) and only at disturbed sites, where it contributed 20.4% (CP40), 7.3% (BL60), and 5.0% (FLM30) of total water-surface CH4 flux.

Stem CO2 was positive across all disturbance classes (overall: 2.7 umol m-2 s-1, CI: 2.4-3.0, n = 486; regenerating highest at 5.1, CI: 4.2-6.2). Leaves were the only component with net CO2 uptake (-1.3, CI: -2.0 to -0.6, n = 19).

## R.3 Stem height and species controls on CH4 transport

Stem CH4 flux decreased with measurement height (p < 0.001; Fig. 3a,b): model-predicted fluxes for *R. mangle* in the wet season were 1.16 nmol m-2 s-1 at the sediment surface and 0.32 nmol m-2 s-1 at 100 cm. Season was also significant (p = 0.04). No height effect was detected for CO2 (p = 0.89; Fig. 3d,e).

Species identity affected CH4 flux (p = 0.003), and the species x height interaction was significant (p < 0.001; Fig. 3c). At 0-50 cm, *Laguncularia racemosa* had the highest flux (5.73 nmol m-2 s-1, CI: 2.34-13.60), followed by *Conocarpus erectus* (1.25, CI: 0.14-3.43), *Rhizophora mangle* (0.63, CI: -0.36 to 2.21), and *Avicennia germinans* (0.59, CI: -0.30 to 1.96). At 100-150 cm, all species converged: *L. racemosa* 0.09, *C. erectus* -0.31, *R. mangle* 0.31, *A. germinans* 0.75 nmol m-2 s-1. Across all heights, *L. racemosa* (1.21, CI: 0.21-3.04) exceeded *R. mangle* (0.45; p = 0.004) and *C. erectus* (0.27; p = 0.03); *A. germinans* was intermediate (0.46, CI: -0.36 to 1.60) and did not differ significantly from other species. For CO2, *L. racemosa* was also highest and *C. erectus* lowest (p < 0.001; Fig. 3f).

In a model with species-status combinations (Fig. 3c), dead *A. germinans* had a higher estimated marginal mean (0.75 nmol m-2 s-1, CI: -0.43 to 2.98) than living *A. germinans* (0.49, CI: -0.43 to 1.82; p = 0.13). *R. mangle* showed no alive/dead difference (living 0.48, dead 0.47; p = 0.88). *L. racemosa* had the highest estimated marginal mean (1.24, CI: 0.13-3.46).

## R.4 Bottom-up CH4 budgets: surface area and component partitioning

Terrestrial laser scanning resolved the component surface areas that convert flux densities to stand-level budgets (Fig. 4). Woody (trunk plus branch) surface area was far larger in healthy plots (0.86-0.98 m2 per m2 ground) than in ghost plots (0.17-0.22 m2 per m2 ground; branches comprised the majority of segments in both), and aerial root surface area differed even more sharply: healthy plots supported large living *Rhizophora* prop-root systems (root surface area 413-419 m2 per ~1,800-2,200 m2 plot, 0.19-0.24 m2 per m2 ground), whereas ghost plots retained little live root structure (49-58 m2; 0.02-0.03 m2 per m2 ground). Soil and water surface areas were set by inundation: ghost plots were continuously flooded (water fraction ~1, no exposed soil), while healthy plots exposed soil at low tide and were water-covered at high tide.

Scaling component flux densities (Fig. 2) by these surface areas yields the first component-resolved CH4 budgets for these forests (Fig. 5; tide-averaged, exponential stem-extrapolation scenario). Plot-scale CH4 emission was higher in ghost than healthy forest (ghost mean ~32, range 7.7-74.7 mg CH4 m-2 d-1; healthy mean ~6, range 1.0-17.5 mg CH4 m-2 d-1). Individual ghost plots: CP40 74.7 (95% CI: 45.8-103.9) and FLM30 26.6 (16.5-37.1) mg CH4 m-2 d-1 in the wet season, declining to 20.0 (0.7-40.3) and 7.7 (6.1-9.4) in the dry season. Healthy plots ranged from 1.0-1.5 (SRS5) to 3.5-17.5 (SRS6), the SRS6 wet-season value driven by high soil and root emission.

The budgets are dominated by entirely different pathways in the two states. In ghost forest, the flooded water surface accounted for ~99% of plot CH4 emission, with soils contributing nothing (no exposed sediment) and stems, roots, and coarse woody debris each <1%. In healthy forest, below-ground and below-canopy pathways dominated: soils contributed 69% and aerial roots 23% of plot emission (92% combined), with water surfaces 7% and stems <1%. Thus, although stem CH4 flux densities are elevated in disturbed forest and vary strongly with height and species (Sect. R.3), stems are a negligible term in the whole-ecosystem CH4 budget in both states, because woody surface area per unit ground area is small and stem flux declines with height. The leading emission pathway is instead set by hydrology: standing water over dead, flooded ghost-forest soils, versus exposed and root-permeated sediments in intact forest.

Two features make these budgets robust. First, the plot totals are insensitive to how stem flux is extrapolated above the measured height range: the exponential-decay and zero-above-maximum scenarios differ by <2% (ghost 36.0 vs 36.8; healthy 1.42 vs 1.45 mg CH4 m-2 d-1), a direct consequence of the small stem contribution. Second, because ghost-forest emission is overwhelmingly a water-surface flux, the ghost budget is effectively a direct measurement (diffusive plus ebullitive water flux scaled by inundated area) rather than a structurally extrapolated estimate. The principal structural uncertainty is therefore confined to the healthy class, where soil emission depends on the exposed-soil fraction (tidal state) and root emission on the TLS root surface area.

## R.5 Bottom-up versus top-down budget closure

We compared the bottom-up chamber x TLS budgets (Sect. R.4, R.7) against the top-down airborne fluxes for the two mangrove end-members (Fig. 8). To place the two methods on a common basis, airborne CH4 fluxes were compared directly (both represent near-constant-through-day emission), while airborne CO2 fluxes, measured near midday, were converted to daily means using the site-specific relationship between midday and daily net ecosystem exchange for mangrove forest (Delaria et al. 2024, their Fig. S24a); for ghost forest, with negligible gross primary production, daily and midday CO2 fluxes are approximately equal. Bottom-up campaigns (wet October 2022; dry March 2023) were matched to the airborne October 2022 deployment and to the mean of the February and April 2023 dry-season deployments, respectively.

The top-down fluxes reproduce the qualitative structure of the bottom-up budgets. For CH4, both methods identify ghost forest as the stronger emitter during the wet season and place healthy mangrove and both dry-season end-members near zero. In October 2022, top-down ghost-forest CH4 (51 +/- 27 nmol m-2 s-1) exceeded intact mangrove (29 +/- 16), matching the bottom-up ordering (ghost 36.6, intact 6.9 nmol m-2 s-1); the bottom-up and top-down ghost estimates agree within one standard error, as do both dry-season end-members (bottom-up ghost 10.0 vs top-down 4.5 +/- 10.4; intact 1.6 vs -0.2 +/- 8.6 nmol m-2 s-1). Intact mangrove in the wet season is the one case where the bottom-up estimate (6.9) falls below the top-down value (29 +/- 16), though within the large top-down uncertainty. For CO2, both methods show intact mangrove as a net daily sink (bottom-up -2.9 to -3.9; top-down -2.2 +/- 2 umol m-2 s-1) and ghost forest near zero to a slight source (bottom-up +0.9 to +1.2; top-down -1 to +1 umol m-2 s-1); all end-member comparisons overlap within one standard error.

This agreement is notable given the methods are fully independent -- static chambers scaled by laser-scanned surface area versus airborne eddy covariance disaggregated by footprint -- and share no common measurements or parameters. In particular, both independently resolve the central result of this study: hurricane-killed ghost forest emits more CH4 than intact mangrove while its CO2 sink collapses. This ordering is the opposite of that produced by a preliminary airborne disaggregation that split out a regenerating class, in which the highest-emitting pixels were reassigned away from ghost forest; the two end-member representation, which agrees with the chamber-based budget, was therefore adopted (Sect. R.1). Two caveats temper the CO2 comparison: the airborne-to-daily CO2 conversion relies on a mangrove tower relationship, and the chamber respiration terms were themselves collected near midday and treated as daily means, so the bottom-up CO2 budget carries an unquantified diurnal-scaling uncertainty. The CH4 closure, which requires no such conversion, is correspondingly more robust.

## R.6 Environmental drivers and porewater geochemistry

Porewater geochemistry varied across the disturbance gradient (Fig. 6a,b). A principal components analysis of 11 porewater variables across four sites and five depths explained 83.5% of the variance in three components (PC1: 40.4%, PC2: 27.4%, PC3: 15.8%; Fig. 6b). PC1 was loaded by salinity (+0.43), dissolved CH4 (+0.40), sulfate (+0.37), and d13C-CH4 (+0.36), contrasted with ORP (-0.38) and dissolved oxygen (-0.36). PC2 was loaded by sulfide (+0.49), opposed by iron (-0.43), DOC (-0.41), and alkalinity (-0.36). Ghost (CP40) and regenerating (BL60) sites plotted toward higher PC1 values; healthy sites (SRS5, SRS6) toward lower PC1 values.

Dissolved porewater CH4 was measured at five core sites during two seasonal campaigns (October 2022, March 2023) and a October 2025 sampling at four sites (Fig. 6c). Mean porewater CH4 was highest at FLM30 (73 uM, range 60-86 uM across campaigns), followed by CP40 (36 uM, 4-62 uM), BL60 (29 uM, 14-47 uM), SRS6 (5 uM, 1-10 uM), SRS5 (4 uM, 3-4 uM), and SE1 (0.1 uM).

Porewater salinity was higher at ghost sites (CP40: 50-63 PSU; FLM30: 61-64 PSU) than healthy sites (SRS5: 20 PSU; SRS6: 27-30 PSU), with BL60 intermediate (35-38 PSU) and SE1 lowest (8 PSU; Fig. 6d). Individual sites showed large seasonal variation in dissolved CH4 at relatively stable salinities: CP40 porewater CH4 ranged from 4 uM (wet season) to 62 uM (dry season) while salinity remained between 50 and 63 PSU. Within healthy and regenerating classes, porewater salinity and dissolved CH4 were negatively correlated, consistent with the expected suppression of methanogenesis by sulfate at higher salinities, though not statistically significant (healthy: r = -0.484, p = 0.156, n = 10; regenerating: r = -0.484, p = 0.156, n = 10; Fig. 6e). Within ghost forest, this relationship reversed: porewater salinity and dissolved CH4 were positively correlated (r = 0.604, p = 0.029, n = 13).

## R.7 Net CO2-equivalent forcing across the disturbance gradient

Combining the bottom-up CH4 budget (Sect. R.4) with net ecosystem CO2 exchange yields the net radiative forcing of each disturbance class (Fig. 7). Net CO2 exchange was constructed bottom-up as component respiration minus gross primary production (Sect. 3.4): for healthy forest, respiration from soil, water, roots, and wood (chambers x TLS) plus a literature-based canopy foliar respiration term, minus tower-derived GPP; for ghost forest, where the defoliated canopy fixes negligible carbon, net CO2 exchange equals ecosystem respiration. The resulting healthy-forest net CO2 uptake, -4,706 g CO2 m-2 yr-1 (-1,283 g C m-2 yr-1), closely matches the independent tower-based net ecosystem productivity for this forest (1,170 +/- 127 g C m-2 yr-1; Barr et al. 2010), supporting the bottom-up construction.

Healthy mangrove forest was a strong net radiative sink. Net CO2 uptake (-4,706 g CO2 m-2 yr-1) overwhelmed CH4 emission (2.1 g CH4 m-2 yr-1; +60 g CO2-eq m-2 yr-1 at GWP100, +174 at GWP20), leaving a net forcing of -4,646 g CO2-eq m-2 yr-1 (GWP100) or -4,532 (GWP20). CH4 offset only 1.3% (GWP100) or 3.6% (GWP20) of the CO2 sink.

Ghost forest was a net radiative source. Loss of the photosynthetic canopy turned the CO2 balance positive (+1,442 g CO2 m-2 yr-1), and elevated CH4 emission (11.8 g CH4 m-2 yr-1; +329 g CO2-eq m-2 yr-1 at GWP100, +956 at GWP20) added to the warming, for a net forcing of +1,770 g CO2-eq m-2 yr-1 (GWP100) or +2,398 (GWP20). CH4 accounted for 18.6% (GWP100) or 39.9% (GWP20) of the combined warming -- a far larger share than in healthy forest, reflecting both higher CH4 emission and the collapse of the CO2 sink.

Hurricane conversion of healthy forest to ghost forest therefore switched the ecosystem from a net radiative sink to a net radiative source, a change of approximately 6,400 g CO2-eq m-2 yr-1 (GWP100) or 6,900 (GWP20), of which CH4 contributed roughly 270 (GWP100) to 780 (GWP20) g CO2-eq m-2 yr-1. These budgets are annualized from wet- and dry-season campaign means and, for the healthy class, incorporate tower GPP; a fully independent top-down evaluation of both the CO2 and CH4 terms awaits the airborne (CARAFE) flux comparison (Sect. R.5). Regenerating forest, which showed the highest component-scale CH4 flux densities (Sect. R.2), is not resolved here because it lacks the terrestrial laser scanning needed for stand-level scaling.

Propagating chamber flux variability, the leaf-respiration parameters (Rd25 and leaf area index), and tower GPP through a Monte Carlo simulation (5,000 draws) yields net-forcing 95% confidence intervals of -6,128 to -3,505 g CO2-eq m-2 yr-1 (GWP100) for healthy forest and +1,480 to +2,054 for ghost forest. The two intervals do not overlap each other or zero, so the disturbance-driven switch from a net radiative sink to a net radiative source is robust to these uncertainties.
