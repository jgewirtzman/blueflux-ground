# Speaker notes — MMM7 talk (12 min)
_Keyed to 4_A1_2_Gewirtzman.pptx, main talk S1-30. Times are cumulative targets._

**S1 — Title (0:00)**
"Good [morning]. I'm Jon Gewirtzman from Stanford. Today: how hurricane-driven mangrove die-off can flip these ecosystems from a carbon sink to a methane source."

**S2 — Disturbance threatens mangroves (0:20)**
"Mangroves are among the most carbon-dense ecosystems on Earth — but they're increasingly hit by intensifying tropical cyclones."

**S3-S4 — (visuals) (0:35)**
Let the imagery carry it; one line on the scene.

**S5-S6 — Irma mortality / ponding (Lagomasino 2021) (0:55)**
"Hurricane Irma in 2017 killed vast areas of Florida mangrove — not by wind alone, but by ponding: trapped water drowning the root systems, leaving standing dead 'ghost forest.'"

**S7 — Methane offsets carbon (Poulter 2023) (1:20)**
"And mangroves emit methane, which eats into their carbon benefit. Disturbance should make that worse — but by how much? That's the gap we set out to fill."

**S8 — Collaborators (1:35)**
"This is a big team effort across NASA BlueFlux — quickly, the people who made it possible." (fast)

**S9 — Study sites (1:50)**
"We worked a disturbance gradient in the Everglades: intact, regenerating, and ghost forest."

**S10 — Multi-scale approach (2:10)**
"The core idea: measure at three scales — chambers for pathways, laser scanning for surface area, aircraft for the landscape — so we can both partition sources AND close the budget."

**S11 — Chamber campaigns (2:30)**
"We measured 867 fluxes from seven ecosystem components — soil, water, stems, roots, deadwood, leaves — across wet and dry seasons."

**S12 — Component fluxes (2:55)**
"Soil and water surfaces dominate the per-area rates; stems are the most variable. And everything is elevated in disturbed forest." [Fig: 08_flux_rates_CH4]

**S13 — Diffusion vs ebullition (3:15)**
"Water fluxes are mostly diffusive — but in the wet season, stagnant ponded disturbed sites bubble: ebullition adds ~16% at ghost, 8% at regenerating." [08c]

**S14 — Stem flux vs height (3:35)**
"Stem methane falls sharply with height — a transport signal — and the decline is steeper, from a higher base, in ghost forest." [09f]

**S15 — TLS intro (3:55)**
"To scale those rates we need surface area. We used terrestrial laser scanning and a new automated workflow — Lizzy Powell's — to extract stand structure by component."

**S16 — Disturbance alters surface area (4:15)**
"Disturbance strips surface area and shifts its allocation — far less live stem and root area in ghost forest." [06_tls_surface_areas]

**S17 — Extrapolating tree flux by height (4:35)**
"We fit each stem's flux-height decline and extrapolated it up the full woody profile, above our 1.5 m chamber limit." [09e]

**S18 — Sensitivity to unmeasured height (4:55)**
"That extrapolation only matters in healthy sites — and even there it moves the total by a couple percent, because stems are a small term. Wet vs dry shown." [09e]

**S19 — Area-scaled fluxes (5:15)**
"Scaled to the stand: ghost emissions are almost entirely the flooded water surface; healthy is more distributed — soil and roots." [11f / 11c-e]

**S20 — Total area-scaled emissions (5:40)**
"And the totals are several times higher from ghost forest — driven by that flooded water surface." [11b_budget_stacked]

**S21 — Airborne EC intro (6:00)**
"Chambers give us pathways — but do they add up? Airborne eddy covariance gives an independent top-down flux over the same landscape."

**S22 — Closure (6:25)**
"They agree. Bottom-up and top-down both put ghost above healthy in the wet season — ghost ~37 vs 51 airborne; healthy ~7 vs 29 — overlapping within uncertainty. Two independent methods, same answer." [12a]

**S23 — CO2 mirror (6:50)**
"We ran the identical workflow for CO2 — same chambers, same scaling, same closure." [12b]

**S24 — GHG balance flips (7:15) *** KEY SLIDE ***
"Put it together as radiative forcing. Healthy forest is a strong net sink — matching the tower NEP. Ghost forest flips to a net source. And methane is now about half the warming at the 20-year horizon — versus a couple percent when the forest is alive." [13_gwp_forcing]

**S25 — Porewater (7:45)**
"Why? The porewater tells the story: disturbed sites are higher in dissolved carbon AND salinity." [10_geochem_pca]

**S26 — Hypersaline methane breaks the redox ladder (8:10)**
"And here's the surprise — methane rises WITH salinity in ghost forest, the opposite of the classic sulfate-suppression rule. Disturbance overrides the redox ladder." [10b_salinity_ch4]

**S27 — Data fusion / upscaling (8:35)**
"Pairing the aircraft's spatial reach with tower time series lets us upscale from reflectance — and hindcast and forecast disturbance emissions."

**S28 — Actionable tools (9:00)**
"The goal is actionable: maps and forecasts that put disturbance into blue-carbon accounting."

**S29 — Conclusions + next (9:20)**
"To conclude: hurricane disturbance flips mangroves from a radiative sink to a source. Blue-carbon benefits are sensitive to disturbance state — not fixed. So inventories and nature-based solutions need to include disturbance class and permanence risk. We're extending this to burial and lateral fluxes, updated IPCC factors, and the microbial side of hypersaline methane."

**S30 — Acknowledgments / Let's talk (9:50-10:30)**
"Thank you — and please find me to talk about tree-flux scaling, TLS processing, or multi-scale approaches." [leave up for Q&A]
