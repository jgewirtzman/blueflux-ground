## R.2 Component-specific flux rates and pathway partitioning

We measured 855 CH4 and CO2 fluxes from seven ecosystem components across eight mangrove sites spanning a hurricane disturbance gradient, during wet (October 2022) and dry (March 2023) seasonal campaigns (Fig. 2). CH4 fluxes were overwhelmingly positive across all components, with soils showing the highest mean flux rates (24.7 nmol m-2 s-1, 95% CI: 14.7-37.0, n = 118), followed by water surfaces (16.4, CI: 10.9-22.8, n = 92), stems (7.8, CI: 4.7-11.9, n = 486), and roots (3.3, CI: 0.7-8.1, n = 65). Coarse woody debris emitted at a lower rate (1.1, CI: 0.0-2.6, n = 27), while leaf fluxes were near zero (0.02, CI: 0.01-0.03, n = 19). Only 7.6% of stem measurements and 7.7% of root measurements showed net CH4 uptake; no water surface measurement was negative.

Pneumatophore density, recorded at soil collar locations during the dry season, was not significantly correlated with soil CH4 flux at any site (Spearman rho = -0.23 to 0.64, all p > 0.08, n = 8-22 per site; Fig. S2). Soil CO2 flux showed a significant positive correlation with pneumatophore density at one intact site (SRS6: rho = 0.79, p = 0.006) but not at others (all p > 0.11), suggesting that pneumatophore-mediated gas transport may locally enhance soil respiration but does not consistently drive CH4 emissions across the disturbance gradient.

Disturbance class strongly differentiated CH4 emission rates. Stem CH4 fluxes in ghost forest (11.5, CI: 7.3-16.8, n = 210) and regenerating forest (14.4, CI: 3.5-32.6, n = 90) exceeded those in healthy mangrove (0.5, CI: 0.2-0.9, n = 181) by factors of 23 and 29, respectively. Soil CH4 fluxes followed a similar pattern, with regenerating sites showing the highest rates (78.6, CI: 38.3-126.8, n = 23), followed by ghost (22.0, CI: 8.9-39.6, n = 31) and healthy (6.6, CI: 3.2-11.9, n = 64). Water surface CH4 was elevated at regenerating (34.7, CI: 14.7-60.5, n = 9) and ghost (19.6, CI: 12.3-28.1, n = 60) sites relative to healthy mangrove (0.7, CI: 0.5-0.9, n = 18). These patterns indicate a shift in the dominant CH4 pathway with disturbance: in ghost and regenerating forests, stems and water surfaces become disproportionately large sources relative to their contribution in intact stands.

Seasonal differences were pronounced for stems and water surfaces. Wet-season stem CH4 (14.4, CI: 7.0-24.6, n = 194) was 4.1 times higher than dry-season (3.5, CI: 2.2-4.9, n = 292). Wet-season water CH4 (41.5, CI: 28.2-56.6, n = 27) was 7.0 times higher than dry-season (5.9, CI: 3.5-10.2, n = 65). Soil and water fluxes cannot be directly compared across seasons because inundation regime shifted the partitioning between these components: ghost forest sites had no exposed soil in the wet season (0 soil, 19 water measurements) versus substantial exposed soil in the dry season (31 soil, 41 water), reflecting near-complete wet-season inundation at disturbed sites. Partitioning water-surface CH4 into diffusive and ebullitive components across 75 chamber traces (492 minutes total deployment) revealed that ebullition was exclusively a wet-season phenomenon: six of 23 wet-season traces (26%) contained ebullition events, compared with none of 52 dry-season traces (Fig. S1). Ebullition was concentrated at disturbed sites, with CP40 showing the highest ebullitive fraction (19.3% of total water CH4, 29 events per hour), followed by BL60 (6.5%) and FLM30 (4.4%). Diffusive flux dominated water-surface CH4 emissions year-round, but episodic ebullition contributed up to one-fifth of total water CH4 at disturbed sites during the wet season.

For CO2, stems were a consistent respiratory source across all disturbance classes (overall: 2.7 umol m-2 s-1, CI: 2.4-3.0, n = 486), with regenerating sites showing the highest stem CO2 (5.1, CI: 4.2-6.2, n = 90). Leaves were the only component showing net CO2 uptake (-1.3, CI: -2.0 to -0.6, n = 19), with 84% of leaf measurements negative.

## R.3 Stem height and species controls on CH4 transport

Stem CH4 flux decreased significantly with measurement height above the sediment surface (mixed-effects model with species, height, and season as fixed effects and site as random effect; F(1,258) = 21.6, p < 0.001; Fig. 3). Model-predicted fluxes for R. mangle in the wet season declined from 1.16 nmol m-2 s-1 at the sediment surface to 0.32 nmol m-2 s-1 at 100 cm, a 3.6-fold attenuation. Season was also significant (F(1,257) = 4.3, p = 0.04), with wet-season fluxes higher than dry-season. No significant height effect was detected for CO2 (p = 0.89), consistent with CO2 being produced in situ by stem respiration rather than transported from soils.

Species identity significantly affected CH4 flux (F(3,256) = 4.7, p = 0.003). *Laguncularia racemosa* had the highest estimated marginal mean flux (1.21 nmol m-2 s-1, 95% CI: 0.21-3.04), significantly exceeding *Rhizophora mangle* (0.45, CI: -0.56 to 1.92; pairwise p = 0.004) and *Conocarpus erectus* (0.27, CI: -0.52 to 1.24; p = 0.03). *Avicennia germinans* was intermediate (0.46, CI: -0.36 to 1.60) and did not differ significantly from other species. For CO2, species differences were also significant (F(3,258) = 13.4, p < 0.001), with *L. racemosa* again showing the highest emissions and *C. erectus* the lowest (pairwise p < 0.001).

The species x height interaction was highly significant for CH4 (F(6,239) = 5.9, p < 0.001), indicating that the height gradient varies by species. At 0-50 cm, *L. racemosa* showed the highest fluxes (5.73 nmol m-2 s-1, CI: 2.34-13.60), substantially exceeding other species (*C. erectus*: 1.25, CI: 0.14-3.43; *R. mangle*: 0.63, CI: -0.36 to 2.21; *A. germinans*: 0.59, CI: -0.30 to 1.96). At 100-150 cm, all species converged toward lower values (*L. racemosa*: 0.09; *C. erectus*: -0.31; *R. mangle*: 0.31; *A. germinans*: 0.75 nmol m-2 s-1).

The effect of tree mortality on stem CH4 flux was species-dependent. In a model including species-status combinations, height category, and season (with site as random effect), the species-status grouping was significant (F(5,315) = 3.3, p = 0.007), and height category was highly significant (F(2,434) = 68.4, p < 0.001). Dead *A. germinans* stems had a higher estimated marginal mean (0.75 nmol m-2 s-1, CI: -0.43 to 2.98) than living *A. germinans* (0.49, CI: -0.43 to 1.82), though the pairwise contrast was not significant after Tukey adjustment (p = 0.13). *R. mangle* showed no difference between living (0.48, CI: -0.63 to 2.18) and dead stems (0.47, CI: -0.41 to 1.71; p = 0.88). *L. racemosa*, which was not separated by status in this analysis, had the highest overall emmean (1.24 nmol m-2 s-1, CI: 0.13-3.46).

## R.6 Environmental drivers and porewater geochemistry

Porewater geochemistry varied systematically across the disturbance gradient (Fig. 6). A principal components analysis of 11 porewater variables across four sites and five depths explained 83.5% of the variance in three components (PC1: 40.4%, PC2: 27.4%, PC3: 15.8%). PC1 was primarily loaded by salinity (PSU: +0.43), dissolved CH4 (+0.40), sulfate (+0.37), and d13C-CH4 (+0.36), contrasted with ORP (-0.38) and dissolved oxygen (-0.36). PC2 was loaded by sulfide (+0.49), opposed by iron (-0.43), DOC (-0.41), and alkalinity (-0.36). Sites separated clearly along PC1, with ghost (CP40) and regenerating (BL60) forests plotting toward higher salinity, higher dissolved CH4, and lower redox potential, while healthy mangrove sites (SRS5, SRS6) plotted toward lower salinity and higher ORP.

Dissolved CH4 concentrations were markedly higher at the regenerating site (BL60: 318 uM at 0 cm depth, 106 uM at 90 cm) and ghost site (CP40: 77 uM at 0 cm, 59 uM at 90 cm) than at healthy sites (SRS5: 13 uM at 0 cm; SRS6: 19 uM at 0 cm). Salinity ranged from 17 PSU at SRS5 to 49 PSU at CP40 at 90 cm depth.

## R.7 Net CO2-equivalent forcing across the disturbance gradient

Converting component-level CH4 fluxes to CO2 equivalents, regenerating mangrove emitted CH4 at a rate equivalent to 335 g CO2eq m-2 yr-1 (GWP100 = 27.9) or 975 g CO2eq m-2 yr-1 (GWP20 = 81.2), followed by ghost forest at 192 g CO2eq m-2 yr-1 (GWP100) or 560 g CO2eq m-2 yr-1 (GWP20). Healthy mangrove emitted only 31 g CO2eq m-2 yr-1 (GWP100). Chamber-based CO2 respiration (soil + stems + roots + water, all components) was highest at regenerating sites (7,897 g CO2 m-2 yr-1) and similar between ghost (3,145 g CO2 m-2 yr-1) and healthy (2,912 g CO2 m-2 yr-1) mangrove.

At GWP100, CH4 represented 1.1% of the combined chamber CO2 + CH4 radiative forcing at healthy sites, 4.1% at regenerating sites, and 5.8% at ghost forest sites. At GWP20, these fractions increased to 3.0%, 11.0%, and 15.1%, respectively, underscoring the disproportionate short-term warming contribution of CH4 from disturbed mangroves.
