# Methods

## 2.1 Study system and disturbance gradient

This study was conducted across mangrove forests of the southwest Florida coast within and adjacent to Everglades National Park. Sites were selected to represent three condition classes along a hurricane disturbance gradient: ghost forest, early-regenerating forest, and mature intact forest. The ghost forest sites (FLM30, CP40) experienced extensive tree mortality and defoliation following Hurricane Irma in September 2017, which generated storm surges that caused widespread mangrove drowning and dieback across the region. Five years post-storm, these sites retained standing dead trunks and root structures but showed no canopy recovery or significant regeneration. The early-regenerating site (BL60) was also damaged by Irma but exhibited active recruitment of mangrove seedlings and saplings at the time of sampling, with a developing but low and discontinuous canopy. The mature intact sites (SRS5, SRS6) are tall riverine mangrove forests along the Shark River Slough that sustained comparatively less structural damage from Irma, though the broader landscape has been shaped by a history of major hurricanes including Andrew (1992) and Wilma (2005), which reduced canopy heights below pre-storm levels in some areas.

The dominant mangrove species across all sites are *Rhizophora mangle* (red mangrove), *Avicennia germinans* (black mangrove), *Laguncularia racemosa* (white mangrove), and *Conocarpus erectus* (buttonwood). Species composition and relative abundance vary with geomorphic position, tidal connectivity, and disturbance history. The study region is characterized by a subtropical climate with a wet season from May through October and a dry season from November through April. Average annual rainfall is 1,000 to 1,700 mm, with approximately 70% falling during the wet season. Tidal range, freshwater inputs from the Shark River Slough, and residence time vary across sites, producing gradients in salinity and hydroperiod that influence both vegetation structure and biogeochemical conditions. Additional single-campaign measurements were made at Marco Island (MI), Rookery Bay (RB10), and the SE-1 scrub mangrove ecotone site to provide broader spatial context across different hydrological and salinity settings.

## 2.2 Seasonal campaigns and multi-scale measurement framework

Repeat measurements were conducted during two seasonal campaigns: October 2022, representing the tail end of the wet season when soils were inundated and water levels elevated, and March 2023, representing the dry season with lower water levels and reduced freshwater inputs. Both campaigns included ground-based chamber flux measurements, terrestrial laser scanning, and environmental sampling at the five core sites (BL60, CP40, FLM30, SRS5, SRS6). The CARAFE airborne eddy covariance flights were conducted as part of the broader NASA BlueFlux campaign, which included four deployment periods (April 2022, October 2022, February 2023, and April 2023), each consisting of six to eight flights over approximately 25 flight hours; the October 2022 and February/March 2023 deployments overlapped with our ground campaigns. Hurricane Ian made landfall north of the study region on 28 September 2022, and elevated water levels were observed at eddy covariance tower sites in the weeks following. For a comprehensive description of the overall campaign design, see [ERL paper reference].

The study integrates four nested scales of observation. At the finest scale, static chambers isolate CH4 flux rates from individual ecosystem components: stems at multiple heights, prop roots, pneumatophores, sediments, and surface water. These flux densities, expressed per unit surface area, are then multiplied by component-specific surface areas derived from terrestrial laser scanning to produce stand-level flux estimates for each site. At the ecosystem scale, an eddy covariance tower at SRS-6 provides continuous half-hourly net ecosystem exchange of CO2 and CH4. At the landscape scale, the CARAFE airborne eddy covariance platform measures spatially resolved fluxes across the full disturbance gradient at approximately 1 km resolution. The bottom-up budgets constructed from chambers and TLS are compared to the top-down tower and airborne constraints to assess closure and identify unmeasured or underrepresented flux pathways.

## 3.1 Component flux measurements

CH4 and CO2 fluxes were measured from individual ecosystem components using closed dynamic chambers connected to portable cavity ring-down spectroscopy analyzers (Los Gatos Research Ultra-Portable Greenhouse Gas Analyzer, model GLA131; Picarro G4301) recording dry-mole fractions at 1 Hz (LGR) or 0.17 Hz (Picarro). Three LGR analyzers (LGR1, LGR2, LGR3) and one Picarro were operated in parallel across sites. Manufacturer-specified precision was 0.9 ppb for CH4 and 0.35 ppm for CO2 on the LGR units, and 1.0 ppb and 0.2 ppm respectively on the Picarro.

Chamber designs were adapted to the geometry of each component. Four elliptical stem chamber sizes (designated A through D) accommodated stem diameters from approximately 3 cm to greater than 20 cm, with enclosed surface areas ranging from 40 to 462 cm2. Stem chambers were sealed directly to the bark surface using modeling clay at three standard heights: 0, 50, and 100 cm above the sediment surface, to test for vertical gradients indicative of soil-origin gas transport. Additional measurements were made at 150 cm where stem diameter permitted. Prop root chambers enclosed sections of individual *R. mangle* aerial roots, sealed with clay. Two soil chamber designs were used across campaigns. During the wet season, soil fluxes were primarily measured using open-bottom acrylic cylinders (23.5 cm diameter) inserted directly into the sediment to depths of 30 to 91 cm, then capped with a lid sealed using a closed-cell foam gasket and vacuum grease. During the dry season, fluxes were measured using smaller PVC collars (14.3 or 19.4 cm diameter) inserted approximately 5 cm into the sediment at least one hour prior to sampling, capped with a domed chamber sealed with vacuum grease. Both designs allowed the chamber to seal with minimal downward pressure to avoid degassing sediment or inducing ebullition. Soil measurements included pneumatophores where present within the chamber footprint, and pneumatophore density was recorded at each collar location. Surface water fluxes were measured using floating chambers (19.4 cm diameter, 324.3 cm2 footprint). All chambers were connected to analyzers via closed-loop tubing with inline desiccant (Drierite) and included a ventilation port sealed with a rubber stopper before measurement began. Chambers were tested for leaks using CO2 readings prior to each measurement. Chamber volumes were calculated from physical dimensions for each chamber-analyzer combination, accounting for headspace, collar volume, tubing, desiccant column, and analyzer cell volume. Total system volumes were 0.5-3.1 L for stems, 0.5-2.0 L for roots, 2.1-39.7 L for soil (varying with collar size and cylinder depth), and 4.3 L for floating water chambers.

Measurements were made on all four mangrove species present, including standing dead trunks and snags at ghost forest sites. At each site, a minimum of five stems were measured per species, and ten sediment collars were distributed within each plot. Median incubation times were 180 s for stems, soil, roots, CWD, and leaves, and 300 s for water surfaces (range across all components: 60-1800 s). Ancillary data collected for each stem included diameter at breast height, perimeter at each measurement height, species, alive/dead status, and stem, soil, and air temperature. A total of 867 flux measurements were collected across six ecosystem components: tree stems (n = 486), soil surfaces including pneumatophores (n = 118), water surfaces (n = 92), roots (n = 65), coarse woody debris (n = 27), and leaves (n = 19).

## 3.2 Flux calculation

Fluxes were calculated using the goFlux R package. For each measurement, both a linear model (LM) and the Hutchinson-Mosier nonlinear model (HM) were fitted to the headspace concentration time series. The HM model accounts for nonlinear saturation of concentration buildup in closed chambers, yielding higher initial flux estimates when headspace concentrations approach equilibrium with the source. The best-fit model was selected using the corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc). Flux rates (nmol m-2 s-1 for CH4; umol m-2 s-1 for CO2) were computed from the initial slope of the selected model, corrected for chamber volume, surface area, air temperature, and atmospheric pressure (101.325 kPa). Minimum detectable flux (MDF) thresholds were computed dynamically for each measurement based on chamber volume, analyzer precision, and measurement duration. Sub-detection fluxes were retained in the dataset at their measured values. All measurements were visually inspected using diagnostic trace plots; seven measurements with clear artifacts were excluded.

Water-surface CH4 fluxes were partitioned into diffusive and ebullitive components. Within each chamber deployment, ebullition events were identified as abrupt upward concentration jumps exceeding 0.10 ppm between consecutive analyzer samples, far exceeding the expected inter-sample diffusive accumulation (0.001 to 0.01 ppm). The diffusive flux was estimated by fitting the concentration time series after excluding ebullition points; the ebullitive component was calculated from the cumulative magnitude of all detected jumps. A total of 75 water-surface traces (492 minutes of deployment) were partitioned.

## 3.7 Porewater geochemistry

Porewater was collected at four sites (SRS5, SRS6, BL60, CP40) at five depths (surface, 0, 15, 45, and 90 cm below the sediment surface) using MHE PushPoint porewater samplers during the dry-season campaign. Water temperature, pH, electrical conductivity, dissolved oxygen, and oxidation-reduction potential were measured in the field using a Hanna HI98494 portable multiparameter meter. Sulfide and ferrous iron concentrations were determined colorimetrically in the field immediately after sampling using a Hach DR 900 handheld colorimeter (sulfide: methylene blue method, Hach reagents 1816/1817; iron: FerroVer method, Hach reagent 2105769). Dissolved CH4 and CO2 concentrations and stable carbon isotope ratios (d13C-CH4, d13C-CO2) were measured by headspace equilibration in syringes, with analysis on a Picarro G2201-i cavity ring-down spectrometer equipped with a SAM autosampler and ultra-zero air carrier gas at Yale University. Dissolved organic carbon was measured on a Shimadzu TOC analyzer, major ions (sulfate, chloride, nitrate, phosphate) on a Metrohm ion chromatograph, and total alkalinity by titration, all at the Yale Analytical and Stable Isotope Center (YASIC).

## 3.8 Net radiative forcing

Component-level CH4 fluxes were converted to CO2 equivalents using 100-year and 20-year global warming potentials (GWP100 = 27.9, GWP20 = 81.2; IPCC AR6). CO2 net ecosystem exchange for the intact mangrove class was derived from tower observations at SRS-6. [PLACEHOLDER: For ghost forest and regenerating classes, CO2 NEE was derived from CARAFE disaggregated fluxes.] Net forcing was expressed as Mg CO2-eq ha-1 yr-1 for each condition class, and the disturbance-induced differential was calculated as the difference between intact and ghost forest. A landscape-scale estimate was computed by multiplying the per-hectare differential by the area of mangrove converted to ghost forest by Hurricane Irma, as mapped by Lagomasino et al. (2021).

## 3.9 Statistical analysis

CH4 and CO2 flux values were transformed using the inverse hyperbolic sine function (asinh) prior to modeling. The asinh transformation accommodates both positive and negative values and approximates the natural logarithm for large values while remaining linear near zero. Component-level summary statistics and 95% confidence intervals were computed using nonparametric bootstrap resampling (5000 iterations, percentile method), stratified by component, site, season, and disturbance class.

Species and height effects on stem fluxes were assessed using linear mixed-effects models (lme4/lmerTest) with site as a random intercept. Three model formulations were evaluated: (1) species + measurement height (continuous) + season; (2) species x height category (0-50, 50-100, 100-150 cm) + season; and (3) species-status combinations (alive and dead *A. germinans* and *R. mangle* treated separately; *C. erectus* and *L. racemosa* pooled by species) + height category + season. Models were restricted to the three sites with species-level identification (BL60, SRS5, SRS6; four species with n >= 5 retained). Estimated marginal means were computed on the asinh scale and back-transformed to flux units via the sinh function. Pairwise contrasts used Tukey adjustment; Type III F-tests assessed fixed-effect significance.

Multivariate structure in porewater chemistry was assessed using principal components analysis on 11 variables (salinity, dissolved CH4, sulfate, d13C-CH4, ORP, dissolved O2, sulfide, iron, DOC, alkalinity, dissolved CO2) across all site-depth combinations. Variables were centered and scaled prior to analysis.
